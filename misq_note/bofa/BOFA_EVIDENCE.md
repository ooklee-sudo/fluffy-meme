# Bank of America Institute: spending by household income, 2026 (primary PDFs in `raw/`, downloaded 2026-10-14; text via pdftotext)
Source: Bank of America Institute (Consumer Checkpoint, monthly, and two restaurant reports). Data: BofA aggregated credit and debit card spending per household; households split into **income terciles** (one third each; thresholds not published; "lower income" = lowest tercile; the report says the cut is a quantitative estimate of income). Internal, not public microdata; only published exhibits, not downloadable data.

**Timeline of lower-income findings after the war (28 Feb 2026), quoted from the PDFs.**
- *Consumer Checkpoint, April 2026 (March data).* "Lower-income households' total card spending rose to 2.2% YoY in March ... higher-income households' spending growth was much stronger, up 3.9% YoY, while those in middle-income group gained 2.8%." The rise for lower income "is largely because gasoline makes up a larger share of spending for those with smaller incomes. Consequently, those with lower incomes curbed discretionary purchases (spending outside of groceries, utilities and gasoline) last month, and their YoY spending growth on discretionary goods dropped back relative to increases seen among middle- and higher-income households." Gasoline was "around 8-10% of their total card spending ... roughly twice that of those with higher incomes".
- *Consumer Checkpoint, May 2026 (April data).* "lower- and middle-income households easing back on discretionary spending in April, while their higher-income counterparts continued to power forward"; "spending growth on travel and restaurants improved slightly in April" (all households).
- *Consumer Checkpoint, July 2026 (June data).* Lower-income spending growth 4.8% YoY in June (from 4.1% in May) vs 5.8% for higher income (from 5.4%); "the gap between higher- and lower-income households' spending growth also narrowed significantly and was the smallest we've seen since July 2025".
- *Consumer Checkpoint, August 2026 (July data).* Lower-income growth 5.4% YoY, middle-income 4.9%; discretionary spending also converged.
- *Consumer Checkpoint, October 2026 (September data).* Lower-income total spending +5.5% YoY vs +4.9% (middle) and +4.8% (higher); "the divergences across income cohorts are much smaller than they were in 2025 and the first three months of this year."
- *Restaurants report, 24 August 2026 (data through July).* "Lower-income households are now posting the fastest restaurant spending growth among income groups": restaurant spending lower +4.1% YoY in July vs ~+3.3% for middle and higher; total restaurant spending +3.3% YoY (transactions +1.1%). By type (Exhibit 7): lower-income households are increasing spending at all restaurant types except pizza, which fell for all income groups; for middle income QSR was "more flat", for higher income QSR was flat. National chains "are not fully participating in the rebound", with growth strongest at independents and regional operators.
- *Taste test, 12 February 2026 (pre-war, 2025 data).* "Quick-service restaurants are being hit in part because lower-income consumers are reining in spending. In 2025, these households comprised the largest share of spending at such restaurants (Exhibit 5)". So in BofA data, lower-income households *are* the largest-spending group at QSRs, in contrast to the NCHS per-person frequency data.

**What this says about the proposition "after the war's oil spike, low-income households visit fast-food chains less".**
1. **Supported in the short run (March–April 2026):** lower-income households trimmed discretionary spending in March and April while gasoline took a larger share of their budget; Bank of America reports this directly by income. These data are total discretionary, not fast food.
2. **Not supported from June onward:** lower-income restaurant spending grew fastest by July (+4.1% YoY, highest among cohorts), including at QSR, while gaps across income groups closed by August–September. Wage growth for lower-income households (after-tax) was reported higher than for others from mid-2026.
3. **Pre-war context:** the QSR weakness among lower-income households was already underway in 2025, and the national-chain share loss is to independents/regional, not necessarily to less eating out.
Together with McDonald's own statements (`../oil_income/MCD_INCOME_EVIDENCE.md`) and the null aggregate tests, the evidence is: a transitory squeeze on lower-income discretionary spending in March–April 2026, followed by recovery; no evidence of a lasting fast-food-specific collapse. Caveats: card data from one bank (not representative; lower-income households use cash/other banks more); tercile thresholds unknown; BofA's exhibits are charts (values not extractable except where the text states them); YoY changes mix price and volume.
