# MIS Quarterly Review: Reviewer 1 (theory, digital platforms and social media)

Manuscript: "When Does Executive Speech Move Markets? Platform-Mediated Announcements, Binding Constraints, and the Limits of General Theory"

---

## 1. Summary of the paper

The paper studies 4,838 original posts by the U.S. president on his own platform (March to October 2026), together with hourly futures data, to ask whether executive posts move markets through a general channel (attention/salience, habituation) or only conditionally. The central empirical finding is that during the 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz, posts that two LLM coders classified as de-escalation were followed by a 1.29% fall in WTI within two hours (n = 33), with smaller moves in the dollar, CNH and Treasury futures. Escalation posts and posts on monetary policy or non-tariff China matters show no response, and the result survives a momentum control and exclusion of the two largest events. Three pre-registered "general mechanisms" (binding constraint, salience, habituation) are tested out of sample and none is confirmed, and an AI-conducted "interpretive analysis" of 136 posts yields propositions that also fail held-out tests. The authors conclude that platform-mediated executive speech is a "conditional, announcement-carrying channel" and offer the pre-registered, blind-coded design as their methodological contribution.

## 2. Assessment of theoretical contribution and fit to MISQ

**What is the IS contribution?** I could not identify one. The paper's own framing is that the post is "the policy's first public form" (Section 2.1), so that the post is the news. If so, the finding is that an announcement of a decision that relaxes a physical supply constraint lowers the price of the constrained commodity. That is an application of ordinary information-based price discovery in a single commodity market. Nothing in it depends on the information system. There is no IT artifact, no platform affordance, no feature of the platform's architecture, and no variation in the channel. The platform enters only as the place where the text appeared. The title promises "platform-mediated announcements", but the design never contrasts the platform with any other channel. It does not compare the first post against subsequent wire or official confirmations, against announcements made through press secretaries or leaks, or against the same speaker's decisions before or after a channel change. The two statements in Section 2.1 that would make the platform matter ("lowers the cost of producing messages" and "removes intermediaries that would otherwise filter, summarize, or confirm") are never turned into a testable proposition. The only platform-specific analysis is the arrival process (Section 4.1), and that analysis is disconnected from the market results (see M7).

**Is it new?** The finding that presidential social-media posts and announcements move prices conditionally is well within a literature the paper does not cite. That literature includes studies of presidential tweets and firm values, of tweets and exchange rates, yields and equities, and of company-specific presidential tweets and intraday reactions (e.g., Born, Myers and Clark 2017; Ge, Kurov and Wolfe 2019; and several later studies of Trump tweets and FX or sovereign yields). It also omits the IS and finance work on firm and executive social-media dissemination (e.g., Blankespoor, Miller and White 2014; Chen et al. 2014) and the event-study literature on central-bank and geopolitical announcements, which already distinguishes "news" from "noise" and asymmetric reactions to good and bad news. The related-work section reduces this to Bollen et al. (2011), Tetlock (2007), Sims (2003), Barber and Odean (2008) and Gürkaynak et al. (2005). Bollen et al. is a contested sentiment-prediction paper and is not a theoretical anchor. The authors have not shown what is new relative to the existing work on presidential communication.

**Is theory developed, or only boundary conditions asserted?** The latter. Section 5.2 states: "The paper's main theoretical claim is a boundary condition." A boundary condition requires a theory with a boundary. The three "general mechanisms" in Section 2.2 are one-sentence heuristics, not theories. The salience mechanism (H2) is attributed to Sims (2003), but rational inattention does not predict that reaction rises with the posting count on a topic. It predicts that agents allocate limited attention to the signals that are most informative about payoff-relevant states. The habituation mechanism (H3) is a rational-learning story without a model. Both were then tested on a hold-out sample (Fed and China) in which, in the authors' words, "the hold-out topics show essentially no hourly reaction to posts at all" (Section 4.3). The theory that survives, "announcements that change the state of a binding constraint move the constrained price", was generated post hoc after the pre-registered keyword analysis misled (Section 3.3) and after H1 failed to clear Holm correction (0.056). The speech-act and Habermas apparatus in Section 2.1 (declaratives, directives, validity claims) is never operationalised. The only trace of it is the ACTION/RHETORIC coding, whose result is not reported in the manuscript (see M6). It is decoration. The negative results are presented as a contribution ("it constrains the theories that can reasonably be built on this setting"), but the tests are largely uninformative, as the authors concede (MDE 0.82; MDE 0.75; tests run on topics with no reaction). The paper's contribution to MISQ is therefore (a) a single-war event study, (b) a set of mostly uninformative nulls, and (c) a pre-registration/LLM-coding workflow. None is an IS theory contribution of the kind the journal expects.

**Fit.** The audience is finance/political economy. An IS framing could be possible (platforms as channels for authoritative announcements, speed of information incorporation, platform as a source of non-exogenous event timing), but the manuscript does not build it. The mixed-methods and "LLM as coder" claims are methodological, and the LLM-coder claim is not validated (M5).

## 3. Major concerns

**M1. No platform theory and no platform variable; the title and framing outrun the design.**
*What is wrong.* The claim in the title and in Section 5.2 ("the unit of analysis for platform-mediated executive speech should be the announcement of a decision in a state where a constraint binds, rather than the post") is not tested against any alternative in which the platform plays a role. The observation that many large reactions "occur when the post is itself the first public announcement of the decision" (Section 4.4) is based on "at least four" of the eight largest reactions. That is selection on the dependent variable, with no base rate: how many of the 33 de-escalation posts, or of all 136, were first announcements, and what were their reactions?
*Why it matters.* Without a contrast, "platform-mediated" is a label, and "the post carries the decision" is tautological: a market reacts to news.
*Fix.* (i) Code, for every war-period post, whether the decision was first public on the platform, using the wire timestamps the authors already collected for 20 cases and extending to all 33+28 war posts (first Reuters/Bloomberg/AP headline time versus post time). Then estimate reaction as a function of "first-public-source" and the lead time over the wires. (ii) Compare with later official confirmations of the same decision (White House statement, press briefing) to show whether the post or the confirmation carries the price move. (iii) State explicitly which platform affordance (zero cost, no intermediary, authenticated authority) generates a prediction that a non-platform channel would not, and test it. If the data cannot support this, the paper should be reframed as a finance note, not an IS paper.

**M2. The "binding constraint" mechanism is not measured, the hypotheses are specified inconsistently, and the headline claim was reached after the pre-registered tests failed.**
*What is wrong.* (a) "Binding" is a calendar dummy: "high K = from the war (1 March 2026)" for oil and "from 3 April 2025" for tariffs (RESULTS_PAPER.md). There is no measure of how constrained transit actually was (tanker transits, AIS counts, war-risk premia, backwardation, Brent-WTI spread). (b) H1 as written in Section 2.2 predicts "a larger reaction during the closure than before it, and no reaction when no constraint binds." On that wording the tariff result (a larger escalation response after the April 2025 tariffs) would support H1. But Section 4.3 reports the same result as "the opposite of the predicted sign", because the pre-registration file predicted that escalation should matter more when K is low. The reader cannot reconstruct the logic of the prediction from the manuscript. (c) The oil de-escalation comparison (1.40% vs 0.01%) is significant at p = 0.014 but 0.056 after the authors' own Holm correction, and the other three primary comparisons fail. Yet the abstract, Section 5.1 and the Conclusion present the binding constraint as the finding ("Executive speech... moves prices when it announces a decision that changes the state of a binding constraint"). (d) The "low K" cell for oil pools Iran and other oil-geopolitics posts. Section 4.2 shows that for Russia/Venezuela posts escalation raises oil and de-escalation does nothing; for pre-war Iran posts de-escalation lowers oil by 0.62% (n = 8, agreed labels). The 0.01% "before the war" in Section 4.3 is thus an average over cells that move in opposite directions, and it is not the same pre-war estimate as the one in Section 4.2.
*Why it matters.* A theory-testing paper cannot supply its confirmatory tests with a dummy, an ambiguous hypothesis and a failed multiplicity adjustment, and then claim support for the hypothesis in the discussion.
*Fix.* Replace the dummy with a continuous daily or hourly closure-intensity measure (transits, insurance spreads, term structure) and estimate the interaction with de-escalation posts on the 33+ war events and the pre-war Iran posts. State H1 once, with explicit signs for each cell, and report the pre-registered and exploratory parts in separate tables. Reword the abstract and conclusion to say that the pre-registered H1 test did not survive Holm correction.

**M3. The headline result is exploratory, small-n, fat-tailed, and the event window is contaminated.**
*What is wrong.* (a) The key finding comes from Amendment 1 after the pre-registered keyword classes produced a misleading result (RESULTS.md labels the re-run "Amendment 1; exploratory"). The manuscript says only that the correction "is documented as a dated amendment" and nowhere states that the headline is exploratory. (b) n = 33 events, with 23 March and 7 April (−9.8% and −12.0%) supplying about half the mean. The median is −0.30% and the mean without the two events is −0.67%. Throughout the manuscript the 1.29% figure is quoted as the effect, and the Abstract reports it without the median. (c) Reaction is measured "from the open of the hourly bar containing the post". A post at minute 55 has a window that begins almost an hour before it. The authors' own RESULTS.md notes "exact post-time within the bar is not used", and the manuscript omits this. For a research question about reverse causation (posts respond to developments), starting the window up to an hour before the post is the wrong design. Figure 2 shows the pre-window drifting in the opposite direction from the post-reaction (de-escalation posts follow a rise), which is consistent with posts reacting to prices. (d) The null is "all other bars of the period that contain no keyword-selected post within two hours". News-heavy periods are more volatile than the quiet bars that form the null, so permutation p-values are anti-conservative. The "outside U.S. equity hours" contrast (−2.25% vs −0.39%) is confounded: both mega-events (11:23Z and 22:32Z) fall outside U.S. hours; removing them gives roughly −1.0% for that group. (e) Posts cluster in bursts (Section 4.1), but only a 3-hour same-topic filter is used, and 49 de-escalation posts fall to 33 with WTI bars with no reporting of what was dropped (weekends, closures).
*Why it matters.* The entire empirical case rests on these 33 events, and the manuscript presents the result as robust.
*Fix.* (i) Report median, trimmed mean and sign-test p alongside the mean in the abstract and Section 4.2. (ii) Redo the windows from the close of the bar containing the post (or use the 5-minute data for the 60-day subsample as a validation and say how many events it contains). (iii) Use a null matched on time of day, weekday and trailing volatility (or standardise by a rolling hourly volatility as is already done for hold-out topics). (iv) Cluster by day/week or use wild-cluster bootstrap. (v) Report all dropped posts and why. (vi) Label the finding "exploratory, derived from Amendment 1" wherever it appears.

**M4. The pre-registration claim is overstated, and the negative results do not "constrain theory".**
*What is wrong.* The plans are files in the authors' own repository, written, per Section 3.5, after the authors had already seen parts of the data ("Plans state what the authors had already seen"). They are not time-stamped on an independent registry (OSF, AsPredicted), and the plans were amended after unfavourable results. The three "mechanisms" are a sequence in which each was proposed after the previous one failed ("binding constraint → salience → habituation", RESULTS_OOT.md). That is a specification search with a pre-registration veneer, and the habituation hypothesis itself was inspired by an exploratory in-sample coefficient (−0.24, p = 0.036, uncorrected) that RESULTS_OOT.md calls "exploratory, unreplicated". The tests are also mostly uninformative. (a) H2 is tested on topics (Fed, non-tariff China) with no reaction at all, so there is no variance to explain; "Salience through posting intensity is rejected" does not follow. (b) H3's out-of-time test has an MDE of 0.82 against an effect of 0.24. The manuscript says "neither confirmed nor refuted" in Section 4.3 and then, in Section 5.2, "we report three failures of general theory without ambiguity." These sentences cannot both be right. (c) The Q1 test of the interpretive propositions finds that "the ORIG mean itself is about zero (+0.03)" in the pseudo-out-of-sample set (RESULTS_QUAL_TESTS.md), so there was nothing to distinguish from amplification; the manuscript omits this. (d) The pooled habituation estimate (−0.24) is described in Section 4.3 as "in-sample" but is pooled over all four topics including the two hold-out topics (RESULTS_HOLDOUT.md; the true in-sample figure is −0.28, p = 0.07).
*Why it matters.* The paper's stated third contribution ("a finding that constrains the theories") and the subtitle ("the limits of general theory") are not supported by tests that cannot reject anything.
*Fix.* Replace "rejected/not confirmed" with equivalence tests or confidence intervals expressed in units of the effect of interest, and say for each test what effect size it could have detected. Drop H2 as a test of salience, or construct a salience measure on topics where there is an outcome response (oil, tariffs; the in-sample coefficient is +0.082, p = 0.33). Register future tests on an external registry. Retitle: the "limits of general theory" should be changed to something the data support.

**M5. LLM coders: independence, validity and look-ahead are not established, and "blind to outcomes" is not enough.**
*What is wrong.* Two "independent model instances" of the same model are not independent coders. Their errors are correlated, so kappa (0.75 to 0.96) measures consistency, not validity. There is no human validation yet (Section 3.3, Appendix C is empty), and the primary sample is conditional on coder agreement, which selects clear and prominent posts. The agreed-label restriction is not benign: coder disagreement is correlated with ambiguity, which is related to market relevance. Agreement in the out-of-time oil sample is only 0.62. Models may also have been trained on text that includes the market commentary on the 2022–2024 posts, so "blind" labelling of that sample is not outcome-blind. The interpretive analysis uses "two independent analysts" who are also model instances, and the sample used to read the themes (the 20 triangulated cases) was selected on the dependent variable. Gioia-style stepwise abstraction and Myers and Klein's principles of reflexivity and multiple interpretations presuppose a human interpreter in a context. Claiming trustworthiness through "independent coding, negative-case analysis and an audit trail" for model instances is not credible.
*Why it matters.* The paper's methodological contribution is that LLM coding plus pre-registration yields credible inference. The data in the paper are consistent with exactly the opposite conclusion: the first keyword-based result was wrong, and the final classification is validated only against itself.
*Fix.* Complete the 150-post human subsample before review and report (a) kappa between model-agreed and human labels, (b) the headline estimate on human labels, (c) the estimate on posts where coders disagree (to show what the agreement filter drops). Use different model families for the two coders. Use a recent-knowledge-cutoff check, or compare coding of 2026 posts (after the model's training cutoff) with 2022–2024. Reduce the interpretive section to a clearly labelled exploratory reading, drop the Gioia and Myers and Klein framing, and say that human interpretation was not involved.

**M6. Selective reporting and unexplained changes of sample and hypothesis across files and text.**
*What is wrong.* The pre-registered unified design (RESULTS_PAPER.md) has four predictions (P1 binding constraint, P2 learning, P3 verifiability premium, P4 no unconditional asymmetry). The manuscript reports P1 as H1, relabels the learning prediction as H3, and omits P3 (ACTION vs RHETORIC: oil +0.56 pp, p = 0.12; tariffs −0.08; pooled +0.30, p = 0.20) and P4. Section 3.3 describes classifying "type (action versus rhetoric)", and Section 2.1 builds on speech-act theory, yet no action/rhetoric result appears. The tariff results (one of the three pre-registered cells, large in the tariff-escalation after Liberation Day) are mentioned only in a clause. Different war-period de-escalation estimates appear without reconciliation: 1.29% (n = 33, coder 1), 1.40% (n = 31, agreed labels, Section 4.3), 1.45% (n = 30, Section 4.2), 1.37% (coder 2). Escalation n is 28 in the text and in RESULTS_PRETREND.md, but 27 in Figure 2.
*Why it matters.* A reader who wants to know whether the pre-registered predictions were met needs the full family of tests, not the subset that fits the narrative. The multiple versions of the headline number make the reader uncertain which is the primary estimate.
*Fix.* Add a single table listing every pre-registered prediction (P1–P4 and H2, H3), its status, n, estimate, interval, and whether it was confirmatory or exploratory. State one primary estimate (and its sample) and present the others as robustness checks in one table. Move the tariff, Treasury, dollar and CNH analyses under the same adjusted family.

**M7. The arrival analysis (Section 4.1) is not connected to the argument and is based on a sample the paper itself shows to be mis-measured.**
*What is wrong.* The 108 "S1" posts are keyword-selected "Iran + threat term" posts, and Section 3.3 reports that exactly this keyword class "contained the principal de-escalation posts". The Poisson-Gamma conclusions therefore describe a mixture of threats and de-escalation announcements. The test "with week fixed effects the Pearson dispersion falls to 0.89" fits roughly 32 weekly parameters to 227 daily observations of a sparse count; it does not demonstrate a Gamma-distributed weekly rate (the paper only shows that a weekly rate varies), so "Poisson-Gamma" and "Cox process" are asserted, not tested. The conclusion that this "is a warning against" the exogeneity of posting times is a non sequitur. Overdispersion with a time-varying rate implies that arrivals track the environment but says nothing about the dependence between arrival and returns. The only linking evidence, from poisson_posts/RESULTS.md, is that "22% (S1) and 27% (S2) of signals fall within ±1 day of the 14 news-derived events, versus 17% of all days... only mildly concentrated", which cuts against the claim that timing is informative about the environment. Finally, Section 4.1 ends with "Weekly signal counts do not predict weekly market outcomes", which is uncited to any result file in the results folder I was given. The headline "4,838 original posts" in the abstract describes the denominator of an arrival analysis, not the analysis sample (33 events).
*Why it matters.* Contribution 1 in the Introduction and the second theoretical contribution in Section 5.2 rest on this.
*Fix.* Redo arrivals on the blind-coded signal classes (escalation, de-escalation) separately. Test the Gamma mixing directly (e.g., compare NB/Poisson-Gamma with other mixing distributions, or test whether the weekly rate is predicted by observable war events such as ceasefire talks, tanker attacks or price volatility). Test endogeneity directly: does lagged return or realised volatility predict post arrival (a hazard model of post timing on past price moves)? That would also strengthen the identification section. Replace "4,838 posts" in the abstract by the effective samples.

**M8. Causal language, simultaneity and the identification section.**
*What is wrong.* The title ("Move Markets"), the first contribution and the Section 5.3 conclusions use causal language. The manuscript acknowledges that the momentum control "cannot rule out that the post and the price move respond to the same news at the same moment." The momentum control is also weak: the pre-trend slope in ordinary hours is −0.027 (SE 0.019), so a control that estimates a near-zero relationship will leave the estimate unchanged (1.29 vs 1.32) by construction. The text calls this "rules out momentum", but it only shows ordinary-hour momentum is small. The 20 triangulated cases all show other reported drivers and seven were not mentioned in market coverage; this is stated in Section 4.4 as a finding about the "channel" rather than as a threat to the estimate. The "Identification" paragraph (Section 4.2) reports a first stage that is "strong" at two hours (a trivially mechanical result, since the instrument is the event itself) and null reduced forms at the daily horizon, which adds nothing.
*Fix.* Use causal language only for what the design can support ("followed by"). Add a direct test of simultaneity: for each war-period event, compute wire-headline timing relative to the post (see M1) and drop or separately report events where the wire headline precedes the post. Use implied-volatility or intraday 5-minute tape around posts in the final 60 days to show no pre-post move. Delete or move the IV paragraph.

**M9. The interpretive study is not interpretive research and its inferences are circular.**
*What is wrong.* Selecting 20 cases by the largest and most opposite reactions and then reading market-wire coverage of each, then reporting "every one of the 20... has other reported drivers", is guaranteed to produce confounders in a period dense with news. There is no comparison with randomly drawn posts or with non-event hours. "T4" (posts as decision carriers) is derived and illustrated from the same cases, and the held-out tests (Q1, Q4) are run on a "pseudo-out-of-sample" set consisting of different topics (Fed, China, 2022–2024 daily data) in which reactions are near zero, so a test cannot fail in an informative way. Q2 and Q3, the propositions that were most closely tied to the theoretical argument (counterparty denial, concurrent news), are untestable with existing data. Theme agreement for the two analysts is reported as kappa 0.37 for "claims of agreement" and (from RESULTS_QUAL.md) 0.42 for boasts. The authors state that the themes "were developed" with Gioia's method but note elsewhere "patterns were read after seeing reaction values" (RESULTS_QUAL.md limitations), which the manuscript does not report.
*Fix.* Either cut Section 3.6, 4.4 and 4.5 to a short, explicitly exploratory case appendix, or strengthen it: include a random sample of posts and a matched set of quiet hours in the triangulation, provide base rates for each theme, and run the held-out tests on the same topic and the same market in a later time window (post-14 October 2026 data, now available as prospective collection).

## 4. Minor concerns

1. Introduction: "Seven weeks earlier, and many times since, similar posts had threatened strikes, announced pauses". Seven weeks before 7 April is mid-February, before the war began on 28 February 2026. The only earlier posts discussed are 23 March (two weeks earlier is after, not before, in any case: 23 March is two weeks before 7 April). Fix the timeline.
2. "fell by double digits in the next session" (Introduction) is not what is measured (two-hour window, −12.0%); give the session figure or reword.
3. Abstract says "March–October 2026", Section 3.2 says "1 March and 13 October 2026" for posts, but market data begin October 2024 and the tests use 2022–2024 posts. State all samples in one table (period, n posts, n signal posts, outcome frequency, role: exploratory/confirmatory).
4. The claim that event-study designs "often assume that posting times are exogenous" needs a citation; most Trump-tweet studies already control for confounding news explicitly.
5. Section 4.2 treats S&P +0.23% (p = 0.006, n = 14) and the multi-asset results as supportive, but only Treasuries, DXY and CNH are Holm-adjusted (and across assets only). The Holm family for the whole paper (WTI, Brent, KRW, S&P, Nasdaq, SOX, NVDA, UAL, XOM, ZN, DXY, CNH, gold, times several cells and codings) is not defined.
6. Gold is reported as "did not respond significantly" after de-escalation, but the point estimate is +0.32% (RESULTS_HOLDOUT.md), with a sign opposite to the risk-off logic the dollar and Treasury results are read through. Discuss.
7. The directional mapping "escalation = risk-off: ZN up, gold up, USD up, USD/CNH up" is applied, yet de-escalation raising the S&P while dollar and Treasuries fall is also consistent with a generic risk-on move and not specific to Hormuz. A cross-asset placebo (e.g., post-hours risk-on days unrelated to Iran) would help.
8. The Section 4.2 statement that pre-war results are "the same sign at roughly a quarter of the size" rests on significance in only the both-coders-agree subset (n = 8) and not for either coder alone. Describe it as inconclusive, not a replication.
9. The "Reading" that "Escalation posts show no response" ignores Figure 2, in which the escalation path rises 1 to 1.5% four to seven hours later (acknowledged only in the limitations). The paper then has an asymmetric result only inside an arbitrary two-hour window. A pre-specified window sweep (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 8 hours) is needed.
10. Theory-level asymmetry (de-escalation moves oil, escalation does not) has an obvious Bayesian reading: the market already priced a closed strait, so threats add little and relief adds a lot. That reading uses no platform or speech-act concept and is observationally equivalent. The authors should state it and say what distinguishes their account from it (cheap-talk and credibility literatures, e.g., Crawford and Sobel 1982, are relevant).
11. References: the speech-act and Habermas citations are used once and nothing is derived; either derive testable implications or remove them. The text declares "all references must be checked"; those I checked match standard citations, but the literature on presidential posts is missing (see Section 2 above).
12. The Declarations state that LLMs were used "in drafting and analysis support"; the manuscript should say how much of the analysis code, pre-registration text and the "independent analysts" were AI-generated, and how that affects the claim of independence.
13. Practice implications (Section 5.3) recommend "a signal-detection rule" that "should discriminate between decision-carrying announcements and commentary", but the paper has no out-of-sample trading or detection test, and Q1 (the distinction between decision announcements and amplification) failed. The implication is unsupported.
14. Placeholder text remains (author names, Appendix B and C, repository DOI). The pre-registration appendix lists file names that cannot be inspected by reviewers.
15. Figure 2 x-axis starts at "start of bar containing the post", which hides the sub-hour timing problem discussed in M3. Figure 1 panel B mixes the S1 keyword sample with no indication of the blind-coded classes.

## 6. Recommendation

**Reject (with encouragement to resubmit to a finance/political-economy outlet or, after substantial reconceptualisation, as a new submission).** The submission has a clean, interesting empirical regularity (relief announcements move oil during a physical closure, relative to threats) and commendable candour about failures. But as an MISQ paper it fails on its central requirement. There is no IS theory: the platform is never varied or theorised, the "boundary condition" is a one-line description of how news about a supply constraint affects the price of that supply, and the three "general mechanisms" are straw heuristics whose tests are uninformative by the authors' own power calculations. The empirical core is an exploratory re-classification of 33 events, with half the mean from two days, an event window that starts before the post, a null drawn from quiet hours, and no human validation of LLM coders that are not independent of one another. The pre-registration is self-hosted, was written after data were seen, was amended after failures, and is selectively reported. The interpretive component is neither interpretive nor held-out in any meaningful sense. The nulls are presented as a contribution that "constrains theory" although the tests cannot reject. A revision that would be worth considering would need to (i) build and test a platform-specific proposition using first-public-source timing against wire services and official confirmations (M1), (ii) measure the constraint continuously (M2), (iii) re-estimate the headline result with a post-timing-correct, volatility-matched design and complete human validation (M3, M5), and (iv) remove or recast the claims about general theory and pre-registration (M4, M6). That is more than a major revision of the present data; if the editor prefers to allow it, the minimum standard is a new, differently framed manuscript.
