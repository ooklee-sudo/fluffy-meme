# Extension: replication, other oil-geopolitics posts, and two coders (plan `ANALYSIS_PLAN_EXT.md`; `analysis_ext.py`; `results_ext.json`; coder outputs `posts_coded.json`, `posts_ext_coder1.json`, `posts_all_coder2.json`)
Two-year hourly bars (Oct 2024–Oct 2026) for WTI, Brent, S&P 500, Nasdaq, NVDA, USD/KRW; 428 posts coded by two independent model instances (not human). Reaction = log change from the open of the bar containing the post to the close of the next bar; 3-hour cluster filter; null = other bars of the same period without a coded post within ±2 h; permutation p. 'pre' = 15 Oct 2024–27 Feb 2026, 'war' = from 2 March 2026. Sample W = 2026 Iran posts, A = Iran/Hormuz posts before the war, B = other oil-geopolitics posts (Russia/Ukraine, Venezuela, OPEC/Saudi, etc.).
**H_R3 (coder robustness): supported.** Cohen's kappa between coders 0.75 overall (W 0.75, A 0.70, B 0.75; 83% agreement; collapsing MIXED/OTHER 0.75). Counts differ (coder 2 labels more ESC: 131 vs 105 for coder 1 in the same posts), but the key war-period result is the same: de-escalation posts: WTI −1.29% (coder 1, n = 33), **−1.37% (coder 2, n = 33)**, **−1.45% (posts on which both agree, n = 30)**, p < 0.001 in all; escalation posts: −0.02%, +0.06%, −0.05% (p ≥ 0.76). Brent −1.2% to −1.3%; S&P 500 +0.2% to +0.3% after de-escalation (p ≤ 0.015), +0.05% to +0.13% after escalation (n.s.).
**H_R1 (replication in Iran posts before the war, Sample A): partial.** De-escalation posts: WTI −0.30% (coder 1, n = 7, p = 0.16), −0.28% (coder 2, n = 9, p = 0.13), −0.62% (agreed posts, n = 8, p = 0.008), Brent −0.58% (agreed, p = 0.008); escalation posts: −0.18%, +0.02% (n.s.). The sign and the asymmetry replicate, the magnitude is about a quarter of the war-period effect (oil was less sensitive to Iran news before the war), and significance appears only in the agreed-post subset; sample sizes are 7–9.
**H_R2 (generalization to other oil-geopolitics posts, Sample B): not supported; the asymmetry reverses.** Before the war: de-escalation (peace/sanction relief/more production) posts: WTI +0.05% (n = 25–27, p ≥ 0.6); **escalation posts raise oil: +0.46% (coder 1, n = 12, p = 0.013), +0.37% (coder 2, n = 14, p = 0.024), +0.46% (agreed, n = 12, p = 0.015); Brent +0.40% (p = 0.03)**. So for Russia/Venezuela-type posts, escalation (sanctions, blockades) moves oil up and de-escalation does not move it down; in the 2026 Iran war the pattern is the opposite (de-escalation moves oil down, threats do not up). In the war period, Sample B de-escalation posts (n = 4) show −0.7% to −1.0% (p = 0.08–0.16).
**Pooled WTI regression with sample fixed effects (post-level OLS, label permutation):** coder 1: DEESC −0.58, ESC +0.27, difference −0.85 pp (permutation p = 0.007; n = 123); coder 2: DEESC −0.60, ESC +0.34, difference −0.94 pp (p = 0.0007; n = 139). Pooled across contexts, de-escalation lowers and escalation raises oil, with the war-period de-escalation effect the largest.
**Reading.** The 2026 finding is robust to the coder and replicates directionally before the war, but it is not a general law about Trump's posts: which direction moves oil depends on the context (a ceasefire/reopening of Hormuz matters when supply is physically blocked; sanctions on Russia/Venezuela matter when they threaten supply). This conditional structure is a better research framing than 'asymmetric reaction to presidential posts': market reactions track the posts' implications for physical supply, i.e., a state-dependent response. Limitations: model coders (human validation pending); small subsamples (4–14 per cell outside the war period); hourly bars; keyword-selected posts; posting times are endogenous; no correction for the many cells shown, except that the primary outcome is WTI.
