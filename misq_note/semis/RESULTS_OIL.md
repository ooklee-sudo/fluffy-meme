# Korea's sensitivity to oil prices vs other markets (plan `ANALYSIS_PLAN_OIL.md`; `analysis_oil.py`; `results_oil.json`; exploratory)
WTI: $66.96 (27 Feb) → peak $114.58 (7 Apr) → $96.24 (6 Oct); daily volatility 2.0% pre-war → 4.5% since. Local daily returns on the previous U.S. session's WTI change (coefficient per 1% WTI), with the previous S&P 500 return as control; HAC 5 lags; pre = Jan 2025–Feb 2026, war = 2 Mar–6 Oct 2026.
- **KOSPI:** with the S&P control, oil's coefficient is +0.03 (pre) and +0.09 (war, p = 0.31): no extra effect of oil beyond the U.S. market; oil alone (no control) in the war period −0.09 (p = 0.22). KOSDAQ −0.083 (p = 0.10). Samsung −0.04 (p = 0.66), SK hynix −0.10 (p = 0.36).
- **Other Asian markets, oil alone in the war period:** Nikkei −0.077 (p = 0.05), TAIEX −0.088 (p = 0.04), i.e., about the same as Korea. Korea is not more oil-sensitive than Japan or Taiwan in daily data. The change in the oil coefficient between pre-war and war is not significant for any equity series (p ≥ 0.11).
- **Won (USD/KRW, positive = won weaker):** +0.043 per 1% WTI before the war (p = 0.03), +0.024 with the S&P control in the war period (p = 0.04), +0.051 oil-only (p < 0.001): the one robust oil linkage: oil up → won weaker, present before the war and unchanged. Yet since 27 Feb the won strengthened 6.6% cumulatively despite oil's +44%, so other flows (chip exports, foreign inflows) dominated the level.
**Claim check.** Korea imports essentially all its crude; the Middle East share was about 69–70% early in 2026 (not "almost all"), falling to 48.5% in May–July as alternatives were arranged; reserves of about 200 days. Stock-market evidence: Korea's equities collapsed on the war onset (KOSPI −19% to 31 March) but the daily link to oil moves is no stronger than Japan's or Taiwan's, and the won reacts to oil as before the war. Immediate physical shortage was avoided by reserves and re-sourcing; the immediate hit was financial (equities) and cost-of-living (fuel prices), which these data cannot measure.

## Addendum: WTI vs Korean semiconductor stocks since the war and since June (exploratory; `analysis_oil_semis.py`, `results_oil_semis.json`)
Levels (27 Feb 2026 close = 100 for stocks; WTI in dollars): 27 Feb WTI 67.0; 31 Mar 102.9 (SK hynix 76.1, Samsung 77.2); 7 Apr 114.6 (peak; hynix 86.3); 29 May 91.2 (hynix 219.9, Samsung 146.4); 10–11 Jun 93.7/91.6 (hynix 193/198); **22 Jun WTI 78.9, SK hynix 275.1 (peak), Samsung 163.3**; 30 Jun WTI 70.6 (low), hynix 249.8; 31 Jul 86.2, hynix 161.9; 30 Sep 97.2, hynix 167.4; 6 Oct 96.2, hynix 167.1, Samsung 125.6. So in levels the two move inversely over the whole period: the oil spike coincided with the March crash in chips; oil fell from April to June while chips tripled; since late June oil rose again (+36% from the low to 6 Oct) and SK hynix fell 39% from its peak.
Daily co-movement (previous U.S. session WTI change → next Korean day return; per 1% WTI): since 2 March: SK hynix −0.10 (p = 0.36), Samsung −0.04 (p = 0.66), correlation about −0.05; **since 1 June: SK hynix +0.12 (p = 0.41), Samsung +0.15 (p = 0.21)**, correlation +0.06 to +0.09, with SOX control +0.17 to +0.18 (not significant); since the 22 June peak the same (+0.14 and +0.15, p ≥ 0.2). In March–May the relation was negative (SK hynix −0.27, p = 0.10; KOSPI −0.18, p = 0.08) but disappears when the previous SOX return is controlled (≈ 0). Weekly changes since 1 June (18 weeks): correlation of SK hynix with WTI −0.07, Samsung −0.02.
The eight largest daily WTI moves since 1 June and the next Korean day: 27 Jul WTI −8.5% → SK hynix −15.8%, Samsung −14.4%, KOSPI −11.5% (chips crashed as oil fell); 23 Jul +6.0% → −8.7%; 29 Jul +6.2% → −5.8%; 28 Sep +15.3% → −0.2%; 25 Sep −11.8% → −5.2%; 13 Jul +8.9% → +3.6%; 16 Jun −5.9% → +5.7%; 10 Sep +6.3% → −2.2%. Direction is mixed.
**Reading.** Since June, higher oil prices have not systematically gone with lower Korean chip prices at the daily or weekly frequency; the inverse relation in levels (oil down/chips up in April–June, oil up/chips down since July) is a coincidence of two slow-moving paths, and the largest chip crash (27–28 July) occurred on a day oil fell sharply. Korean chip prices follow the global semiconductor cycle (SOX) far more than oil. The March crash coincided with the oil spike but was also the first reaction to the war itself.
