# 2022 gasoline surge and state restaurant employment, driving-exposure design (plan `ANALYSIS_PLAN_EMP2022.md`; script `analysis_emp2022.py`; output `results_emp2022.json`)
Panel: 47 states (IA, NM, SD, WV have no NAICS-722 series on FRED; DC missing), 28 months (Sept 2021–Dec 2023); ln restaurant employment (CES, NSA); fixed effects: state × calendar month, year-month, state-specific linear trends; E1 = standardized 2019 VMT per resident × ln monthly gasoline price; SEs clustered by state; permutation p from 2,000 draws (500 for secondary outcomes; planned 5,000).

**Primary (H_E1), formally satisfied.** β = **−0.0106** (se 0.0041; cluster p = 0.012; permutation two-sided p = 0.011, lower-tail 0.006): in a state with 1 SD higher driving, a 10% higher gasoline price goes with ≈0.1% lower restaurant employment (−0.0106 × ln 1.10 ≈ −0.10%); moving gasoline from $3.0 to $4.5 (ln 1.5 = 0.405) implies ≈ −0.43% for a +1 SD state.
**Specificity (H_E2) not clean.** Total nonfarm: β = −0.0039 (cluster p = 0.28; perm p = 0.050); restaurant share of nonfarm: −0.0067 (cluster p = 0.12; perm p = 0.064). Roughly a third to a half of the restaurant effect appears in total employment, so it is partly a general labor-market difference between high-driving states.
**Poverty exposure (H_E3) not supported:** restaurants +0.0024 (p = 0.73), nonfarm +0.0016, share +0.0008.
**Checks.** Gas averaged over the previous 3 months: −0.0153 (p = 0.001); dropping five largest states: −0.0094 (p = 0.026). **Not robust:** without state trends +0.0068 (p = 0.52); restricting the window to 2022-01..2023-12: +0.0052 (p = 0.56). **Placebo (gasoline path shifted 24 months earlier) gives +0.0150 (p = 0.003)**, i.e., a significant effect of the opposite sign; the plan's rule (placebo not significant with the same sign) is technically met, but the placebo shows that this design can generate systematic relationships between driving intensity and restaurant employment unrelated to the 2022 gasoline prices.

**Reading.** The pre-registered primary test is negative and significant, but the result depends on the early part of the window (September–December 2021, when states were still recovering at different speeds from COVID and the Delta wave) and on state-specific trends; it disappears when those are changed, the placebo is significant, and part of it is general employment. I would report it as fragile, not as evidence that gasoline prices reduced restaurant jobs. Consistent with the card-spending result (no restaurant effect, `RESULTS_STATE2022.md`).
