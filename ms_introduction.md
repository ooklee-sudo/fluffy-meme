## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourced leaderboards have become one of the main ways AI models are compared in practice. The most influential of them, Chatbot Arena (now LMArena), ranks models from millions of pairwise votes by ordinary visitors (Chiang et al. 2024). A visitor reads two anonymous responses to their prompt, votes, and only then learns which models produced them. The votes are aggregated with a Bradley–Terry model (Bradley and Terry 1952) into a ranking that developers cite in product launches and that investors and the press treat as a summary of competitive position. Model developers also train on preference data of this kind, so the platform's votes shape the models as well as describe them.

The value of this signal rests on one design feature: blinding. Evaluators are not told which model wrote each response, so that the aggregate of their votes reflects the quality of the responses and not the reputation of the developer. Decades of evidence on brand effects explain why the platform needs this rule. Identity cues change evaluations (Allison and Uhl 1964) and even experienced quality (Plassmann et al. 2008). Much of the platform's methodological work, such as style control for response length and formatting (Dubois et al. 2024), is an attempt to remove information that evaluators should not be pricing.

Blinding on Arena has a gap that the platform does not control. Language models often introduce themselves without being asked ("I'm Claude, an AI assistant made by Anthropic"), so the evaluator sometimes learns one model's identity before voting. The leak comes from the evaluated party itself. How often a model does this is set by its developer through training, and the leak shows up in the transcripts the platform releases. This paper asks three questions. First, does a model's unprompted disclosure of its own identity change evaluator votes, holding response quality fixed? Second, why? Third, is the effect large enough to matter for the rankings and the preference data the platform publishes?

**What we find.** The usual prediction is that revealing a strong brand helps. Brand research predicts favoritism toward well-known identities. Disclosure theory predicts that parties with favorable identities will reveal them (Grossman 1981; Milgrom 1981). We find the opposite. Using 135,624 Arena votes from April to July 2025, we identify 606 conversations in which exactly one model disclosed its identity unprompted. A disclosing model is no more likely to win, but it is five to seven percentage points more likely to lose, mostly at the expense of ties, and its net vote margin is about nine points lower. The penalty does not favor market leaders; if anything, they bear more of it. It is concentrated in disclosures that interrupt an answer, where the identity is least relevant to the user's request.

**Identification.** Disclosure is not randomly assigned. Disclosing conversations have shorter and simpler prompts, and different models disclose at different rates. So we do not claim that disclosure is as good as random. Our design rests on three checks that do not depend on that assumption, and none of them uses a language-model judge.

1. *Placebo look-alikes.* We train a classifier that predicts from prompt and context features whether a conversation will contain a disclosure (out-of-sample AUC 0.95). Conversations the classifier flags as likely to contain a disclosure, but in which the model stayed silent, carry no penalty. Comparing disclosing conversations only with these look-alikes reproduces the main estimate.
2. *Saturated fixed effects.* The estimate is stable under fixed effects for the ordered model pair, prompt cluster, and language, and under model-by-prompt-cluster fixed effects. These absorb any model-specific tendency to disclose on particular kinds of prompts.
3. *Inference.* The estimate is not driven by any single developer and holds under randomization inference.

**Why it matters.** The penalty barely moves today's leaderboard, but only because disclosures occur in under one percent of conversations. Per conversation, a disclosure costs as much as making a response about 54 percent shorter. That is the length bias the platform built style control to remove. At a disclosure rate of about seven percent, a typical top-20 model would lose a rank. Developers set that rate through training, and the platform does not adjust for it. The same votes, released as preference data, also penalize generic statements that a response comes from an AI. Models trained on this data would therefore learn not to make statements that AI transparency rules increasingly require. Earlier public releases of the platform contain almost no disclosures, which is consistent with the keyword filtering the platform has described. In other words, the released data may already be shaped by an unreported screening rule.

**Mechanism.** We interpret the evidence with a simple account built on the persuasion knowledge model (Friestad and Wright 1994) and on conversational relevance (Grice 1975). On a platform whose design announces that identity should not matter, an unsolicited self-introduction is irrelevant to the user's request and easy to read as an attempt to trade on brand. Evaluators then treat the disclosure as a signal of the party's *conduct* rather than as information about its *quality*. We call this the conduct reading. It makes three predictions that standard brand and disclosure accounts do not:

- The effect of disclosure is negative under a blind format.
- The penalty does not rise with the value of the brand.
- The penalty disappears when disclosure is solicited or identity is admissible.

Expectancy-violation and contextual-norm accounts can explain why a disclosure might feel out of place. They do not, on their own, predict that the sign depends on the platform's admissibility rule rather than on the content disclosed. Our randomization of that rule separates the conduct reading from these alternatives (Table 1, Section 2).

### 1.1 Contributions

**Platform design and the integrity of evaluation signals.** Research on online evaluation systems traces distortions of the aggregate signal to two sources (Dellarocas 2003; Mayzlin et al. 2014; Luca and Zervas 2016). One is manipulation by the evaluated party, such as fake reviews. The other is evaluators' own biases. Recent work on AI leaderboards follows the same split. It studies selective reporting of private test results (Singh et al. 2025), adversarial voting (Min et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025), and stylistic biases (Dubois et al. 2024). We document a third source that falls between these two. The evaluated party talks directly to evaluators through a channel the platform did not sanction. The platform's own blinding rule, not evaluators' views of any brand, then decides how that message is priced. Two design implications follow. First, blinding is incomplete whenever evaluated parties can identify themselves in the content being judged, and the resulting distortion is a component of the ranking that the evaluated party controls. Second, the direction of the distortion is a design choice. The same disclosure hurts under a blind rule and helps where identity is admissible. Platforms can measure this component, adjust for it the way they adjust for style, or remove it at the source. Because we quantify the distortion in units of a bias the platform already corrects, we give operators a direct benchmark for whether such an adjustment is worth making.

**Human versus model judges in AI evaluation.** Language-model judges are increasingly used in place of human raters, often justified by high average agreement with human preferences (Zheng et al. 2023). We provide field and experimental evidence of a systematic divergence on a specific, policy-relevant feature. Human evaluators penalize real-world self-disclosures. Model judges do not. This divergence would not show up in aggregate agreement statistics, because disclosures are rare. Yet it is large enough per instance to affect rankings at plausible disclosure rates. It is also directly relevant to preference-based training: data from human votes carries a penalty on AI self-identification that data from model judges does not.

**A boundary condition for voluntary disclosure.** Disclosure theory predicts that parties with favorable identities gain from revealing them, because silence is read as concealment (Grossman 1981; Milgrom 1981; Dranove and Jin 2010). We show that this prediction holds only where identity is admissible. Where a platform rule declares identity out of bounds, the same volunteered disclosure carries a penalty that does not rise with the value of the identity. The comparison in Table 1 separates two dimensions that prior work has typically combined: who supplies the identity cue (a third party or the evaluated party) and whether the format treats identity as admissible. Our setting is the one cell that prior work has not studied. The boundary condition should extend to other blind evaluation settings where evaluated parties can identify themselves, such as double-blind peer review, anonymous marketplaces, and blind auditions.

Section 7 discusses implications for platform design and AI evaluation practice.
