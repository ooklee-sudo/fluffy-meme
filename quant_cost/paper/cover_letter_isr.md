We submit the manuscript "Vivid Incidents and Flat Probability Responses: How Managers Weigh Privacy Risk When Choosing Between Cloud AI Services and Local Language Models" as a Research Article that empirically tests theory. The intended audience is academics and practitioners who study or govern AI sourcing.

## Contribution statement

**Contribution to the literature.** Research on sourcing generative AI has focused on cost: break-even analyses compare on-premises language models with commercial services, and real-options models treat adoption as a timing problem. Behavioral work on security spending shows that decision makers favor prevention and respond to threats too small to justify spending. What remains unknown is whether managers' choice between a cloud service and a local model depends on how a privacy risk is presented, apart from how large it is. We address this with a preregistered experiment in which 152 professionals involved in technology purchasing made four sourcing choices and stated how much they would pay each year to remove the cloud provider's exposure risk. The normative benchmark is explicit, so we can ask not only whether a vivid report of an exposure at another firm shifts willingness to pay, but also whether it changes how willingness to pay responds to the stated probability, which separates an availability account from an affect-based one. Participants who read the incident report were willing to pay about a third more, and they responded less to the stated probability, although this second result is borderline. The report did not raise participants' general estimate of exposure probability, which does not support a simple availability account. A stylized analysis of the buyer's break-even and of a vendor's incentive to reduce real risk shows what a flat response to probability would imply for the market for AI services. We also show that a large share of participants computed the normative willingness to pay exactly, so that mean-based tests of sensitivity summarize a mixture of calculators and non-calculators. The scenario figures are calibrated on our own measurements of a quantized open model and a cloud model, and we release the code and materials. We report the effects with their uncertainty, including a null result for the performance format and a primary result that weakens when respondents bound by prohibiting policies are excluded.

**Contribution to practice and policy.** The findings bear on how organizations communicate security incidents internally and how vendors present model performance. An incident narrative can shift sourcing decisions without any change in the underlying risk, so governance processes that rely on a cost–risk comparison should place probabilities and losses next to the narrative. The experiment also gives managers a benchmark for the comparison: the local model's net operating disadvantage against the expected exposure loss, with a measured example of how cost per correct answer depends on utilization.
