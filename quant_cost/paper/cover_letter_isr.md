The intended audience is academics and practitioners who study or govern AI sourcing.

## Contribution statement

**Contribution to the literature.** Research on sourcing generative AI has focused on cost and technology: break-even analyses compare on-premises language models with commercial services, and real-options models treat adoption as a timing problem. Behavioral research on security decisions shows that people respond to how a risk is presented, but it has not asked what such a response implies for the structure of AI sourcing. We argue that this structure is distinctive. The in-house option is a tunable technology with a measurable quality penalty, its cost advantage depends on how heavily it is used, and the privacy risk is a rare event of a recent technology whose vendor's security effort buyers rarely observe. We show how a presentation-sensitive weight on risk acts on this structure. In a preregistered experiment with 152 professionals, with scenarios calibrated on our own measurements of a quantized open model and a cloud model and an explicit normative benchmark, a vivid report of an exposure at another firm raised willingness to pay by about a third and, more tentatively, flattened its response to the stated probability, without raising participants' general probability estimates. A stylized model then shows how such a response moves the break-even between local and cloud sourcing, how costly the shift is when the local option's advantage is thin, and why it weakens vendors' reward for reducing real risk, including under vendor competition and when only some buyers calculate. We report the uncertainty openly: the primary result is borderline, the performance-format effect is inconclusive, about 40% of the participants computed the normative value exactly, which points to differences in how managers decide and motivates our model of mixed buyers, and the market implications are implications of assumptions, not findings. We do not claim that the psychological effects are specific to AI; the contribution is how they operate on the structure of AI sourcing.

**Contribution to practice and policy.** The findings bear on how organizations communicate security incidents internally and how vendors present model performance. An incident narrative can shift sourcing decisions without any change in the underlying risk, so governance processes that rely on a cost–risk comparison should place probabilities and losses next to the narrative. The experiment also gives managers a benchmark for the comparison: the local model's net operating disadvantage against the expected exposure loss, with a measured example of how cost per correct answer depends on utilization.
