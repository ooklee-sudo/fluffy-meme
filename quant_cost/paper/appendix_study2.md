## Appendix A. Normative benchmark and scenario values

For each business case, B is the yearly running-cost saving of the local model, E the extra yearly error cost of the local model, and L the loss from an exposure, all in thousands of dollars. The local model's net advantage is (B − E) + λL, the normative willingness to pay (WTP) to eliminate the API's exposure risk is λL, and the break-even exposure probability is λ* = (E − B)/L = 4% in every case. The scale factor m is 0.8 for the inquiry case, 0.6 for the HR case, 1.0 for the contract case and 1.2 for the claims case.

*Table A1. Scenario values and normative benchmarks.*

| Case | B | E | L | Net advantage at λ = 2% | Normative choice at 2% | Net advantage at λ = 6% | Normative choice at 6% | WTP* at 2% | WTP* at 6% | ln ratio | Response limit (L/4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Sorting customer inquiries | 80 | 144 | 1,600 | −32.0 | cloud API | +32.0 | local | 32.0 | 96.0 | 1.099 | 400 |
| B. Extracting fields from HR records | 60 | 108 | 1,200 | −24.0 | cloud API | +24.0 | local | 24.0 | 72.0 | 1.099 | 300 |
| C. Extracting key terms from contracts | 100 | 180 | 2,000 | −40.0 | cloud API | +40.0 | local | 40.0 | 120.0 | 1.099 | 500 |
| D. Triaging insurance claims | 120 | 216 | 2,400 | −48.0 | cloud API | +48.0 | local | 48.0 | 144.0 | 1.099 | 600 |

(Computed with `normative_check_study2.py`; the last column is the largest willingness to pay a participant could enter.) Because λ and L enter only through the product λL and the break-even probability is the same in every case, the normative log ratio of willingness to pay at 6% to willingness to pay at 2% equals ln 3 = 1.099 for every participant, which is the benchmark for S.

## Appendix B. Calibration of the performance figures

**Purpose.** The performance table in each scenario reports accuracy and cost per correct answer for a local model and a cloud model. We calibrated these numbers on our own measurements, using synthetic tasks as proxies for the four business cases. The synthetic tasks are not the participants' organizations' data; they serve to give the figures a documented, reproducible source. The amounts B, E and L are fixed design values and do not depend on these measurements.

**Tasks.** Three synthetic tasks were generated by `tasks.py`: classification of short customer tickets into topic categories (short output), extraction of personal-data fields from short records (structured output), and multi-step arithmetic. The scenarios use the first two: the inquiry and claims cases use the classification task, and the HR and contract cases use the extraction task. The arithmetic task, which has long outputs, was measured but not used in the scenarios. Both models were run on 500 items per task for the scenario figures.

**Cloud model.** Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct served through OpenRouter, queried at temperature 0 on 20 November 2026 at prices of $0.36 per million input tokens and $0.40 per million output tokens. With 500 items per task, accuracy was 99.2% for classification and 100.0% for extraction. Mean prompts were about 80 tokens (classification) and 163 tokens (extraction), with mean completions of 1 and 36 tokens. The implied cost per million correct answers is $29.5 for classification and $73.1 for extraction, matching the figures shown to participants. Under this option, an average of three personal-data entities per extraction request left the firm.

**Local model.** Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (3 billion parameters), quantized to 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) and run through the Hugging Face transformers library with bitsandbytes, on a laptop GPU with 8 GB of memory under Windows with WSL. The GPU was an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Laptop GPU (8 GB memory, maximum power limit 90 W, driver 591.74), the model weights occupied 1.9 GiB, and peak GPU memory use was about 2.4 GB. Idle GPU power was 7.7 W. The measurement also covered 16-bit, 8-bit and 4-bit (FP4) versions of the 3B model and 4-bit versions of the 7B model; only the NF4 3B model entered the scenarios. The run log shows that the 4-bit NF4 version of the 3B model was named explicitly as the local model for the scenario figures, and it was then re-measured with 500 items per task to narrow the intervals. [State why NF4 was chosen.] All measurements were run on 20 November 2026; a first check with a 0.5B model on 10 items tested only that the pipeline worked. Items were run at temperature 0 and seed 0, with 500 items per task. Accuracy is the share of fully correct items, with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Latency, time to first token and tokens per second were recorded per item. On 500 items per task the local model reached 93.6% accuracy on classification (Wilson 95% CI 91.1 to 95.4) and 99.2% on extraction (98.0 to 99.7), against 99.2% (98.0 to 99.7) and 100.0% (99.2 to 100.0) for the cloud model. The scenarios show these as 94% and 99% against 99% and 100%.

*Table B1. Configurations measured in the first sweep (100 items per task; accuracy with Wilson 95% intervals; latency per request; cost per million correct answers at 50% utilization).*

| Model | Bits | Classification accuracy | Extraction accuracy | Latency, classification / extraction (s) | Cost per million correct, classification / extraction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cloud (Qwen2.5-72B) | – | 99% [94.6, 99.8] | 100% [96.3, 100] | 0.28 / 1.21 | $29.44 / $73.05 |

| Qwen2.5-3B | 4 (NF4) | 90% [82.6, 94.5] | 99% [94.6, 99.8] | 0.06 / 0.90 | $2.48 / $31.13 |
| Qwen2.5-3B | 4 (FP4) | 96% [90.2, 98.4] | 92% [85.0, 95.9] | 0.07 / 0.94 | $2.38 / $34.74 |
| Qwen2.5-7B | 4 (NF4) | 99% [94.6, 99.8] | 94% [87.5, 97.2] | 0.12 / 0.99 | $4.16 / $36.70 |
| Qwen2.5-7B | 4 (FP4) | 99% [94.6, 99.8] | 100% [96.3, 100] | 0.13 / 1.02 | $4.42 / $35.24 |

With 100 items the intervals are wide, and accuracy does not fall monotonically with the number of bits; for example, the 4-bit 3B model scored 90% on classification in this sweep but 93.6% when re-run with 500 items, and the other differences in the table are mostly within the intervals. The sweep also shows that on this software stack quantization reduced memory use but not latency: the 4-bit models were about as fast as the 16-bit model, and the 8-bit model was several times slower, so its cost per correct answer was higher. We therefore do not claim a cost advantage for lower precision, only that the 3B model fits in 8 GB at every precision and that its cost per correct answer is below the cloud's in these tasks.

**Energy.** GPU power was sampled through `nvidia-smi`. CPU energy could not be read from the hardware under WSL, so we assumed 15 W for the CPU while a request was running, which makes the energy figures approximate. A wall-socket power meter would be the most defensible measurement. Energy per request multiplied by the electricity price ($0.12 per kWh) gives the electricity cost. Energy is a small part of the local cost; amortization of the hardware dominates (Appendix C).

## Appendix C. Cost model and sensitivity to utilization

**Levelized cost (shown to participants).** For the local model, cost per request = electricity cost + latency × hardware cost per busy second, where hardware cost per busy second = price / (service life × seconds per year × utilization). We assumed a $1,500 machine, a three-year service life and 50% utilization, meaning the machine is available for this workload half of its lifetime. Cost per correct answer = cost per request / accuracy. For the cloud model, cost per request = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price. We report cost per one million correct answers.

**Why 50% utilization.** It describes a machine dedicated to the workload during working hours. Lower utilization raises the hardware share of the local cost in proportion; at 25%, the local cost advantage for the extraction task nearly disappears.

*Table C1. Local cost per million correct answers at different utilization levels (cloud cost unchanged).* At 25% utilization the local cost for the extraction task is only about 12% below the cloud cost.

| Task | Cloud | Local at 25% | Local at 50% (shown) | Local at 75% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classification (cases A, D) | $29.52 | $4.58 | $2.39 | $1.66 |
| Extraction (cases B, C) | $73.09 | $64.17 | $33.27 | $22.98 |

## Appendix D. Marginal cost and fixed versus variable break-even

The cost shown to participants is deliberately the levelized cost, which is conservative for the local option. Two other views are reported here.

**Marginal cost.** For an organization that already owns suitable hardware, the cost of one more request is the electricity cost alone. This view favors the local option strongly and is the other extreme from the levelized view. On the NF4 measurements, the energy-only cost per million correct answers is $0.204 for classification and $2.38 for extraction, against $29.52 and $73.09 for the cloud model.

**Fixed versus variable break-even.** Let F be the one-time setup cost of building the local pipeline, and c_api and c_local the variable cost per request. The break-even volume is n* = F / (c_api − c_local). With F = $2,000 as a placeholder for engineering time, break-even is reached after 51.6 million requests for classification and 21.2 million for extraction when only the hardware is counted, and after 120.3 million and 49.5 million requests when the $2,000 setup cost is added. These figures use the API price minus the local energy cost per request and contain no privacy term. One laptop at 50% utilization can serve about 733 million classification requests or about 49 million extraction requests over its three-year life, so for extraction the break-even volume with setup labor (49.5 million) exceeds what one machine can process. These figures exclude setup labor beyond F, maintenance, staff, and model updates, and they describe one laptop, so they should not be read as the cost of a server-grade deployment. They reinforce a point made in the main text: the local option's advantage depends strongly on volume and utilization.
