45             1. Introduction
46
47             Fluent, well-organized prose has long served as a signal of effort in scholarly publishing. Large
48
49             language models make that signal cheap. A model can draft an abstract, a methods section, or a
50
51             plausible set of results in a minute, and the output is often good enough to survive a quick read.
52
53
               For publishers, preprint servers, and research-integrity offices, the question is therefore no longer
54
55
56
57
58
                                                                  1

1
2
3    whether machine-written papers exist. It is how much to spend on finding them, given that every
4
5
6    check costs money, every flag costs an investigation, and every miss carries a loss.
7
8
9    The detection literature mostly answers a different question, namely how accurately a classifier
10
11   separates machine from human text. Accuracy figures are reported for one generator at a time,
12
13   without a base rate, without the cost of a false alarm or a miss, and without asking whether
14
15
16   authors will adapt once they know how they are being screened. Those omissions matter for any
17
18   organization that has to decide what to deploy. A detector with an excellent hit rate can still be a
19
20   poor investment if machine papers are rare and its false alarms swamp its detections, and a
21
22
23
     detector that works today may stop working as soon as its method is published.
24

26

27
28   stage screen: a free statistical screen scores every document, and only the highest-scoring
29
30   documents are sent to a costly second stage, an LLM judge (Zheng et al. 2023). We then extend
31
32

33

36

38
39   power. To make the model concrete, we calibrate it with data. The free screen is built from
40
41
42   counts of small linguistic events in English scientific papers, such as parentheses, semicolons,
43
44   and passive clauses, which we model as a count process. The calibration data are 474 human-
45
46   written papers from 2015–2019 and matched papers from three generators: GPT-4o, Claude, and
47

50             2. Background
51
52             Screening under asymmetric information. When one party knows more about quality than the
53
54             other, the less informed party must rely on signals and screens. Akerlof (1970) showed that
55
56
57
58
                                                                 3
59
60                                           https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
                                                                                                             Page 4 of 32


1
2
3    quality uncertainty can unravel a market, and Spence (1973) showed that a signal separates types
4
5
6    only if it is costly enough that low-quality types cannot imitate it. Reviewing the literature,
7
8    Connelly et al. (2011) stress the same logic: a signal is informative to the extent that it is costly
9
10   to fake. For scholarly publishing, fluent writing used to be a costly signal of effort, and language
11
12
13
     models have lowered that cost. Organizations are left with screens built on traces that authors do
14
15   not choose deliberately, and the economic question is how much to invest in them and how long
16
17   they stay informative once authors respond.
18
19
20   Detecting machine-generated text. Existing detectors include supervised classifiers, methods
21
22
23
     that use a language model’s own probabilities (for example, Gehrmann et al. 2019; Mitchell et
24
25   al. 2023), and judge-style evaluation by a second model (Zheng et al. 2023). Embedding-based
26
27   comparisons such as MAUVE (Pillutla et al. 2021) and BERTScore (Zhang et al. 2020) measure
28
29
     whether sets of texts mean similar things and say little about fine-grained behavior. Most
30
31
32   detectors are opaque to the person who must act on them, and many deteriorate when the
33
34   generator changes. We use a deliberately transparent screen so that every quantity traces back to
35
36   a countable feature.
37
38
39   Information quality and design. Research on information quality treats accuracy and credibility
40
41
42   as properties an organization must monitor (Wang and Strong 1996), and the design-science
43
44   tradition judges an artifact by whether it solves an organizational problem at acceptable cost
45
46   (Hevner et al. 2004). We follow that orientation: cost per screened document, base rates, and
47
48
49
     adaptive behavior are first-class elements of the analysis.
50
51
52   Count data and dispersion. For counts of independent events in fixed windows, the Poisson
53
54   model implies a dispersion index, the variance divided by the mean, of one. Real text rarely
55
56   satisfies this, because topics, sections, and formatting conventions make events cluster. Count-
57
58

4
5

7
8              clustered a document’s events are.

12             3. A Model of Cascaded Screening and Evasion
13
14             3.1 Setting
15
16             An organization receives a stream of documents, a share π of which are machine-written. A free
17
18
19
               screen assigns each document a score s, with likelihood ratio ℓ(s) = fM(s) / fH(s), where fM and fH
20
21             are the score densities of machine and human papers; we assume ℓ is nondecreasing in s. Given a
22
23             base rate π, the posterior probability that a document with score s is machine-written is p(s; π) =
24
25             πℓ(s) / (πℓ(s) + 1 − π).
26
27
28
               The organization may send a document to a judge. A judged document is flagged with
29
30
31             probability J if it is machine-written (the judge’s detection rate) and a if it is human (its false-
32
33             alarm rate), independently of s given the class. Flagged documents are investigated. Four costs
34
35             apply: cJ, the price of one judge call; cR, the cost of investigating one flagged paper, assumed to
36
37
38             settle its status; L, the loss from each machine paper that is not flagged; and nothing for the
39
40             screen itself. Documents that are not judged are not flagged. A cascade is therefore a depth: the
41
42             screen score above which documents are judged.
43
44
45             3.2 When to call the judge
46
47             Proposition 1 (when to call the judge). Sending a document with posterior p to the judge
48
49
50
               reduces expected cost, relative to not sending it, by V(p) = p·[(L − cR)J + cR·a] − (cJ + cR·a).
51
52             Hence it is worth sending if and only if p ≥ τ, where τ = (cJ + cR·a) / ((L − cR)J + cR·a). If (L −
53
54             cR)J ≤ cJ, then τ ≥ 1 and the judge is never worth calling.
55
56
57
58
                                                                   5
59
60                                             https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
                                                                                                             Page 6 of 32


1
2
3    Proof. Not sending leaves expected cost L·p. Sending costs cJ + cR(pJ + (1 − p)a) + L·p(1 − J).
4
5
6    The difference is V(p), which is linear and increasing in p. ∎
7
8
9    Equivalently, a document should be judged if and only if the screen’s likelihood ratio satisfies
10
11   ℓ(s) ≥ ℓ* = ((1 − π)/π)·τ/(1 − τ).
12
13
14   Proposition 2 (depth). The set of documents worth judging, {s : ℓ(s) ≥ ℓ*}, expands as ℓ* falls,
15
16   and ℓ* is decreasing in π. The cost-minimizing depth is therefore nondecreasing in the base rate
17
18
19   π. It is also nondecreasing in J and L and nonincreasing in cJ and cR.
20
21
22   Proof. τ is increasing in cJ; τ falls with J and with L; and ∂τ/∂cR = [a·L·J + cJ(J − a)] / D², where
23
24   D is the denominator of τ, which is positive when J > a. ℓ* is increasing in τ and decreasing in π,
25
26   and ℓ is nondecreasing in s. ∎
27
28
29
     Proposition 3 (blind spots). τ rises as the judge’s detection rate J falls, and the judge is never
30
31
32   worth calling when J ≤ cJ/(L − cR). If the screen cannot tell generators apart, the relevant
33
34   detection rate for a stream that mixes generators with weights wg is the weighted mean J̄ = Σ
35
36   wg·Jg, so a generator that the judge cannot detect lowers the judge’s value for the whole stream.
37
38
39   Proposition 4 (precision at low base rates). Among the documents a judge flags in an
40
41
42   unscreened stream, the share that is machine-written is πJ / (πJ + (1 − π)a). It is low when π is
43
44   small, even for a judge with a small false-alarm rate. Screening before judging raises the
45
46   effective base rate among judged documents, which raises this share and reduces wasted
47
48
49   investigations.

52   3.3 Strategic evasion
53
54   The cascade model treats authors as passive. Suppose instead that a machine-using author
55
56   chooses an evasion effort e in [0, 1] at cost (κ/2)e², which lowers the probability of detection to
57
58
                                                         6
59
60                                   https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
Page 7 of 32


1
2
3              d(e), with d(0) = d₀ the rate against a non-adaptive author. The author gains B if the paper passes
4
5
6              and loses P if it is detected, so u(e) = B(1 − d(e)) − P·d(e) − (κ/2)e². Let m = (B + P)/κ be the
7
8              ratio of the stakes to the cost of effort. The author chooses e to minimize m·d(e) + e²/2, and the
9
10             organization, anticipating this, cares about the detection rate d(e*) that remains in equilibrium.
11
12
13             Proposition 5 (evasion rises with the stakes). If d is differentiable, decreasing, and convex, the
14
15
16             optimal effort e* satisfies m·|d′(e*)| = e*, or e* = 1 at the corner. Effort is therefore
17
18             nondecreasing in m and in the steepness of d, so equilibrium detection d(e*) falls as the stakes
19
20             rise or evasion becomes cheaper.
21
22
23             Proposition 6 (the detectability paradox). If d(e) = d₀(1 − e), then e* = min(1, m·d₀) and d(e*)
24
25
26             = d₀(1 − m·d₀) whenever m·d₀ ≤ 1. Equilibrium detection is maximized at d₀ = 1/(2m), where it
27
28             equals 1/(4m), and decreases in d₀ beyond that point. A screen that is very effective against non-
29
30             adaptive authors provokes more evasion, and past a point it detects less than a weaker screen
31
32
33             would.
34
35
36             Proof. The first-order condition gives e = m·d₀. Substituting gives d₀(1 − m·d₀), whose
37
38             derivative in d₀ is 1 − 2m·d₀. ∎
39
40
41             Proposition 7 (robust design). Consider two screens built from different feature sets, with
42
43
               detection curves dA(e) and dB(e), where dB is flat in e because its features cannot be edited
44
45
46             cheaply. If dB(0) < dA(0) but dA falls steeply, there is a threshold m̂ such that the screen built on
47
48             B has higher equilibrium detection for every m > m̂. An organization should prefer features that
49
50             are costly to manipulate even at some cost in raw accuracy. This is the logic of costly signals
51
52
53             (Spence 1973) applied to the screen’s inputs.

4
5    The model’s propositions guide the empirical work. We calibrate Propositions 1 to 4 with the
6

23             4.3 Events, windows, and the screen
24
25             Each document is cut into consecutive windows of 500 whitespace-delimited tokens, and only
26
27
28
               the first four windows (2,000 tokens) are kept for every document, human or machine, so that
29
30             length cannot drive any result. In each window we count seven events: parentheses, square
31
32             brackets, semicolons, paired quotation marks, a list of fourteen discourse markers (for example,
33
34             however, therefore, moreover), passive constructions (a form of be plus a participle, found by
35
36
37             pattern matching), and nominalizations (words ending in -tion, -ment, or -ity). The patterns for
38
39             passives and nominalizations are approximate and misclassify some words. For each document
40
41             and event we compute the mean count per window (the rate) and the dispersion index, and the
42
43
44             baseline screen is a logistic regression on these fourteen quantities. A document-level Poisson
45
46             test compares (n − 1) times the dispersion index with a chi-squared distribution; with only four
47
48             windows per document it has low power, so rejection rates understate departures from Poisson
49
50
51
               behavior.
52
53
54
               A dispersion index at one window size says whether events cluster but not how. We therefore
55
56             developed a scale-dependent clustering model. We treat the occurrences of each event as a
57
58
                                                                  9
59
60                                            https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
                                                                                                            Page 10 of 32


1
2
3    doubly stochastic Poisson process, or Cox process: events arrive as a Poisson process whose rate
4
5
6    varies along the document. For such a process, the Fano factor in a window of w tokens is F(w)
7
8    = 1 + Var(Λw) / E(Λw), where Λw is the rate integrated over the window. If rate fluctuations are
9
10   positively correlated over long ranges, as when citations gather in an introduction or passives in a
11
12
13
     methods section, F(w) grows with w; a pure Poisson process gives F(w) = 1 at every scale. We
14
15   estimate F(w) for each event at four window sizes (50, 100, 200, and 400 tokens) within the
16
17   same 2,000 tokens, and summarize each event by its rate, the mean log Fano factor across scales,
18
19
     and the slope of log F(w) on log w. Estimation is by moment matching and not a full likelihood
20
21
22   fit; Fano factors are clipped to [0.05, 20] and set to one when an event does not occur. This
23
24   yields 21 features. The comparison with the baseline was fixed before the model was run, no
25
26   setting was tuned on these data, and the model was designed after we had seen the baseline
27
28
29   results, a limitation we return to in Section 7.

10              6. Discussion
11
12              The central lesson is that detection accuracy is the wrong quantity to optimize in isolation. For an
13
14              organization, the value of a screen depends on the base rate, on the cost of false alarms and
15
16
17
                misses, and on whether authors respond. Each of these shifts the answer in ways that headline
18
19              accuracy hides. Our model identifies how, and the calibration shows that the effects are large.
20
21
22              First, the base rate and the judge’s false alarms matter more than the judge’s price. A judge with
23
24              a 2% false-alarm rate produces mostly false alarms when machine papers are rare, and the
25
26
27
                screen’s economic contribution is to raise the base rate the judge sees. This is why a cascade
28
29              saves money at low base rates, and why the saving comes from fewer investigations and not
30
31              from lower judge fees. Second, blind spots dominate. The strong judge was excellent on two
32
33              generators and almost useless on Claude, and the weaker judge was wrong in the wrong direction
34
35
36              on Claude. A screening organization cannot know which kind of generator it faces, so the
37
38              relevant detection rate is a weighted average that a single blind spot drags down. The cheap
39
40              screen, by contrast, was weaker than the strong judge on two generators and stronger on the
41
42
43              third, at a cost orders of magnitude lower, and a simple rank average of the two did better than
44
45              either alone.
46
47
48              Third, a screen whose features are known is fragile. A crude edit of five easily changed events
49
50              cut detection from 53% to 8%, whereas a generic LLM rewrite did not evade the screen and for
51
52
53
                one generator made detection easier, so the fragility lies in knowing the features and not in
54
55              rewriting power as such. Publishing the features, as research does, supplies that knowledge. The
56
57
58
                                                                  25
59
60                                            https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
                                                                                                            Page 26 of 32


1
2
3    model shows why the edit result should be expected: when evasion is cheap relative to the
4
5
6    stakes, equilibrium detection is far below the figure that non-adaptive authors would produce.
7
8    The detectability paradox means that a very effective screen can provoke enough evasion to
9
10   underperform a weaker one. Designing a screen therefore means choosing features that are costly
11
12
13
     to manipulate, not only features that discriminate. In our data, passives and nominalizations
14
15   played that role, which echoes the economic logic that a signal is informative only to the extent
16
17   that it is costly to fake (Spence 1973; Connelly et al. 2011). Because we publish our method, the
18
19
     relevant benchmark for any deployed version is the equilibrium detection rate, not the headline
20
21
22   accuracy in this paper. The judge, by contrast, was not fooled by the attacks that defeated the
23
24   screen, neither the edit alone nor the hybrid, which suggests that the two stages of a cascade
25
26   differ in robustness as well as in price. A cascade that gates the judge with a known, editable
27
28
29   screen inherits the screen’s fragility, and one that gates it with features that are costly to edit
30
31   would not.
32
33
34   Fourth, accuracy does not transfer across generators, and a screen that needs no examples of
35
36   machine text does not work in our setting. Both findings point to maintenance as the dominant
37
38
39   cost of any screen: it must be recalibrated as generators change and as authors adapt. We
40
41   therefore see these screens as a low-cost first filter whose output is routed to a judge or to human
42
43   review, and not as grounds for action on their own. The explainability of count-based features
44
45
46
     can help an editor discuss a flag with an author, but we have not tested whether it improves
47
48   reviewers’ decisions.

                7. Limitations and Future Research
4
5               The analysis rests on three generators and one genre, English-language scientific papers, so the
6
7
8               results may not carry over to other models, languages, or text types. The sharp drop in transfer
9
10              between GPT-4o and Claude is itself a warning that future releases may differ. Samples are
11
12              modest: the Claude sample (152) is smaller than the GPT-4o (245) and Llama (202) samples
13
14
15
                because we capped generation to limit cost, bootstrap intervals for cross-generator AUCs are
16
17              about ±0.05, and the judged subset of 500 documents is small enough that the cascade and fusion
18
19              results should be read as indicative. The confirmatory test of the clustering model uses one held-
20
21
                out generator. The model was designed after we had seen baseline results, and the development
22
23
24              comparisons involve several paired tests without correction for multiplicity.
25
26
27              Several features of the data construction could affect the results. We generated papers in four
28
29              requests to reach analyzable length, which may introduce structural artifacts such as inflated
30
31              passive dispersion and is not how most users would produce a paper. Human papers come from
32
33
34              two extraction pipelines while machine papers are plain text, although medicine-only results
35
36              suggest that extraction artifacts do not drive the judge comparison. Our prompts asked for in-text
37
38              citations and a given structure, so rate differences such as those in square brackets may reflect
39
40
41              these instructions. Four windows per document give low power, and passive and nominalization
42
43              detection rely on patterns, not a parser.
44
45
46              The economic model is simple. It assumes that investigating a flagged paper settles its status at a
47
48              fixed cost, which is generous given that human reviewers often cannot distinguish machine text,
49
50
51
                and that capacity is unlimited. The costs of review and of a missed paper and the base rate are
52
53              assumed, not measured, so the cost results are structural illustrations and not estimates for any
54
55              organization. The judge’s operating point is fixed at a threshold of 50. The strategic extension
56
57
58
                                                                   27
59
60                                             https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
                                                                                                              Page 28 of 32


1
2
3    considers a single decision by a single author, takes the base rate as given, and uses a quadratic
4
5
6    cost of effort. The programmatic adversarial test is crude; it is a lower bound on an adaptive
7
8    author, who could edit passives and nominalizations too, or optimize against the detector’s
9
10   weights. The generic LLM rewrite used a single attacker (Llama), a single prompt, and the
11
12
13
     clustering screen only. The hybrid of rewriting and feature-aware editing was no stronger than
14
15   the edit alone, but neither attack edits passives and nominalizations or optimizes against the
16
17   detector’s weights, so an adaptive author could do better than any of our attacks. The hard-to-edit
18
19
     feature set was chosen because our attack left it untouched, so its robustness is conditional on
20
21
22   that attack. The judge was evaluated on edit-only and hybrid-attacked papers but not on rewrite-
23
24   only papers, with one attacker and the Gemini judge only; the GPT-4o judge was not evaluated
25
26   on attacked papers. The judge saw edits that an adaptive author could conceal better, and a
27
28
29   human reader might notice them. We did not run MAUVE or BERTScore, we did not test light
30
31   human editing of machine text, and we did not test whether explainable evidence improves
32
33   human decisions.
34
35
36   Four steps would turn this pilot into a fuller test. The first is a stronger adversary: an attack that
37
38
39   also edits passives and nominalizations or optimizes against the detector’s weights, evaluated
40
41   against both the screen and the judges, with rewrite-only papers also given to the judge, and with
42
43   more than one attacker model. The second is to measure the parameters the model needs, with
44
45
46
     data on the base rate of machine-written submissions, on the cost of investigating a flag, and on
47
48   the accuracy of human review, which would replace our illustrative values. The third is to add
49
50   generators, especially open-weight models and future releases, and to replace the Poisson
51
52
     benchmark with a negative binomial reference and the moment estimator with a full likelihood.
53
54
55   The fourth is a randomized experiment in which domain experts classify real and fabricated
56
57
58
                                                         28
59
60                                   https://pubsonline.informs.org/journal/isre
Page 29 of 32


1
2
3               papers with and without an explainable indicator, which would test whether the transparency of
4
5
6               count-based features improves decisions.
7
8
9
10              8. Conclusion
11
12              Whether to screen for machine-written scholarly text is an economic decision, and the value of a
13
14              screen depends on three things that detection accuracy leaves out: the base rate of machine
15
16
17
                papers, the cost of false alarms and misses, and how authors respond. In a model of cascaded
18
19              screening, a free statistical screen is worth most because it raises the base rate that an expensive
20
21              judge sees, a judge with a blind spot is worth little, and a screen whose features are known can
22
23
                lose most of its power through evasion. Calibrated on three generators, the cheap screen detected
24
25
26              machine papers well only within a generator, a strong LLM judge missed one generator almost
27
28              completely, a simple cascade kept most of the judge’s detection at a fraction of its cost when
29
30              machine papers were rare, and a crude feature-aware edit cut the screen’s detection from 53% to
31
32
33              8%, whereas a generic LLM rewrite did not evade it. The same edit, alone or after a rewrite, did
34
35              not fool the judge, and the cascade, which depends on the screen, lost much of its detection when
36
37              attacked. Organizations should therefore evaluate screens by their equilibrium performance
38
39
40
                against adapting authors, favor features that are costly to manipulate, and plan to maintain them.
41
42              The evidence comes from three generators, one genre, and two judges, and a stronger adversary
43
44              and measured cost parameters would be needed to turn these illustrations into estimates.
