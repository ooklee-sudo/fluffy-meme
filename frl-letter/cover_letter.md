Dear Editor,

We submit our manuscript, "Do LLM Labels Mislead Event Studies? Presidential Posts and Oil Prices," for consideration in *Finance Research Letters*.

Event studies increasingly classify news and social media text with large language models (LLMs), and agreement between repeated model runs is often reported as evidence that the labels are reliable. Our paper shows that this evidence can be misleading. We study 191 posts by the U.S. president during the 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz, labelled by five LLM runs and by human coders, and link them to tick-level oil prices.

The paper addresses a methodological challenge for empirical finance and the replicability of text-based event studies:

1. **Consistency is not validity.** The model runs agree with one another at κ = 0.69–0.84 but with human coders at only κ = 0.44–0.49. They over-label escalation even when given the human coders' codebook.
2. **The bias has an identifiable source.** We write the model-label event-study estimate as an exact mixture of human-class means. The decomposition shows that every model label set understates the oil response to escalation posts because it adds de-escalation posts to the escalation class, not because correctly labelled posts respond less. Label sets with similar agreement statistics can bias the estimate by different amounts.
3. **Correction is costly at event-study scale.** Design-based corrections remove the bias but, with few events, need human labels for a third to a half of posts before they improve accuracy.

The paper closes with four reporting practices for event studies that use LLM labels. We are explicit about its limits: one episode and one market, ten human-labelled escalation events, and no causal claim about posts and prices.
