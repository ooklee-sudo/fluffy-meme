We submit our manuscript, "Do LLM Labels Mislead Event Studies? Presidential Posts and Oil Prices," for consideration in *Finance Research Letters*.

Event studies increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to classify news and social media text, and the reliability of these labels is usually judged by how well repeated model runs agree. Our paper shows that this evidence can mislead. We examine 191 posts by the U.S. president during the 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz, labelled by five LLM runs and by human coders, and match them to tick-level oil prices.

The paper speaks to a methodological problem in empirical finance and to the replicability of text-based event studies. It makes three points:

1. **Consistency is not validity.** The model runs agree with one another at κ = 0.69–0.84, but with human coders at only κ = 0.44–0.49. They classify too many posts as escalation, even when given the coders' own codebook.
2. **The bias has an identifiable source.** We write the model-label event-study estimate as an exact weighted average of human-class means. Every model label set understates the oil response to escalation posts, and the decomposition shows why: the models add de-escalation posts to the escalation class, while correctly labelled posts respond as strongly as before. Label sets with similar agreement statistics can bias the estimate by quite different amounts.
3. **Correction is costly at event-study scale.** Design-based corrections remove the bias, but with few events they need human labels for a third to a half of the posts before they improve accuracy.

The paper ends with four reporting practices for event studies that use LLM labels. We are explicit about its limitations: it covers one episode and one market, rests on ten human-labelled escalation events, and makes no causal claim about posts and prices.
