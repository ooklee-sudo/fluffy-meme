## 1. Introduction

Suppose a customer asks a retailer's AI agent to return an item. The agent looks up the order, and the order system replies: *order not found*. The agent now has three options. It can accept the reply and tell the customer that no such order exists; it can look again by another route; or it can hand the case to a person. In our experiments one commercial model, GPT-6.1-terra, took the third option far more often when the "not found" message was false. It transferred the case in 73% of retail runs, against 32% when nothing was wrong, and every such transfer joins a queue of cases waiting for a reviewer.

This paper is about the decision behind that queue. When should a task be taken back from an AI agent, who should make that call, and how many people does the answer require?

One response is that firms should retrain or redesign their agents so that they make fewer errors. They should, but this does not remove the problem. Agents are often bought rather than built, the tools they call are run by systems that the firm does not control, and even a very reliable agent will now and then receive a wrong answer from a database or an interface. What the firm does control is the rest of the system: when it steps in, whether it checks results, and how many reviewers it employs. These choices interact, and a firm that tunes one while ignoring the others will misjudge its costs.

We model a firm that sends tasks to an AI agent. At each step the agent may make errors, the task may be completed, and the firm may hand the task to a human reviewer. Because the data tell the two apart, we give an error two possible consequences. It may *derail* the task, so that the task ends with a wrong output that is costly to detect and repair. Or it may lead the agent to *transfer* the task to a person on its own, which costs a reviewer's time. Reviewers are scarce, and tasks that reach them have to wait. The firm chooses its hand-off rule and decides whether to pay for a check on finished outputs; in an extension it also chooses how many reviewers to employ.

The model has three main implications. First, the firm should count errors by what they cause. The best rule hands off when a weighted count of errors reaches a threshold, and each error type is weighted by the probability that it leads to a wrong output. A type that never does should not be counted at all, however often it occurs. The agent's own transfers barely move this threshold, but they change the load on reviewers a great deal and reduce the value of checking outputs. Second, verification and escalation are substitutes. Better verification raises the number of errors the firm should tolerate, and it is worth most when people are hardest to reach; monitoring, by contrast, is worth most for tasks at the margin of delegation, where handing off costs about as much as letting the agent finish. Third, a firm that decides whether to escalate by looking at the average wait it observes will hand off too often, because each new case also delays the cases behind it. This is the classical congestion externality applied to hand-off rules, and we treat it as a supporting result.

For the evidence we use two sources. A public corpus of 80,036 trajectories of an open-source software-engineering agent shows strong clustering of errors, most of which disappears once tasks are allowed to differ in difficulty. We then ran two randomized fault-injection experiments, one in customer service and one in code repair, with five agents and 11,352 runs. At a random point of each run we inserted either a transient failure or a misleading "not found" message. Transient failures did not change the success of the two commercial models, but a misleading message did. It raised GPT-6.1-terra's transfer rate from 0.40 to 0.74 across customer-service tasks (from 0.32 to 0.73 in retail, where the effect is concentrated, and from 0.58 to 0.75 in airline) and lowered its success rate from 0.77 to 0.46. The additional failures were almost all transfers, not wrong outputs. Claude Sonnet 5.5 behaved differently. It rarely transferred more, but it sometimes finished with a wrong output: we estimate that a misleading message leads to one with probability about 0.12 across customer service and 0.15 to 0.18 in retail. We detect none in the 50 airline tasks, which are too few to rule it out. For DeepSeek-V3.1 the split between the two channels depends on how they are separated. In code repair, where the agent can run tests, the effects were smaller.

We make three contributions. First, existing capacity models of oversight take the demand for review as given, either as a function of an exogenous risk score (Wang and Rachev 2026) or of exogenous reviewer error rates (Lin and Zhang 2026). Our fault-injection results suggest that, for a multistep agent, the volume of escalations depends on the accuracy of its tools: a misleading message raised one agent's transfer rate from 0.40 to 0.74 (from 0.32 to 0.73 in retail). This is evidence from one agent, mainly in one domain, and we do not claim that it holds in general. Second, work on human–AI delegation has mostly asked whether to delegate. We ask when, and by whom, a delegation should be reversed once the agent acts over many steps. Operations research already studies capacity-dependent deferral from classifiers and screening judges (Lykouris and Weng 2024; Wang and Rachev 2026; Lin and Zhang 2026); we add the error structure of multistep agents and evidence on what agents do after an error. Third, the results bear on practice. Because the quality of tool outputs drives escalation volume, data governance and staffing turn out to be one decision. A small injection experiment of the kind we run is enough to calibrate the rule, and a common shortcut, setting escalation thresholds from the observed queue, escalates too much.

A word on how our reading of the data changed. We first treated the whole fall in success after a misleading message as wrong outputs. The transcripts show that for two of the three agents most of it is the agent's own transfer. We therefore report both channels and show where the conclusions depend on how they are separated (Section 5).

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 presents the model, and Section 4 derives the results on thresholds, verification, and reviewer capacity. Section 5 reports the observational and experimental evidence and separates the two channels. Section 6 discusses implications and limitations, and Section 7 concludes. Proofs and details of the experiments are in the appendices.
