## 2. Related Literature

Research on information systems has long asked how work is divided between people and technology. Baird and Maruping (2021) treat delegation to agentic artifacts as an ongoing allocation of rights and responsibilities, Berente et al. (2021) describe the management of AI as a balance between autonomy, learning, and inscrutability, and Fügener et al. (2022) show that people delegate poorly to AI. These papers ask whether to delegate. We ask when, and by whom, a delegation should be reversed once an agent acts over many steps.

The closest work in operations and machine learning studies when to send cases to scarce human reviewers. Lykouris and Weng (2024) consider an AI that classifies items and sends uncertain ones to a congested human queue, where feedback arrives late because of the congestion and is selectively sampled, and they give a near-optimal online-learning algorithm. Their admission rule sends an item to review when the loss that review avoids exceeds the congestion externality that the admission imposes on later items, which they estimate by the number of waiting items of the same type. This is the planner's marginal-cost logic that our Proposition 4 contrasts with a rule based on the average wait; in the parts we read, they do not compare the two. Their object is a classifier acting on independent items of known types, with a reviewer who observes the true cost.

Wang and Rachev (2026) model a controller that decides, for each arriving task with a risk score, whether to automate it or to escalate it to a finite-capacity human queue, while the reliability of the automation drifts as a Markov chain. They prove that the optimal rule is a threshold on the risk score that rises with the backlog ("congestion shedding"), falls under drift ("safety buffering"), and is driven by a shadow price of capacity, and they identify a capacity phase transition. Their introduction states the congestion externality and the planner's shadow price explicitly. This overlaps with our capacity analysis, and we do not present that part as new. Their decisions are made one task at a time on an exogenous risk score; they do not model errors that accumulate within a run, errors with different consequences, transfers initiated by the agent, or verification, and their numerical study is synthetic.

Lin and Zhang (2026) study agents that draft pull requests, a mandatory human approval, and an optional AI reviewer that filters first but whose false rejections send good work back for another agent run. In a queueing network with rework, the optimal share of work sent to review passes through four phases as maintainer capacity grows. Reviewer error rates are exogenous inputs calibrated on a coding benchmark. Their reviewer screens finished outputs, and nothing happens inside the run. Their finding that the value of oversight is non-monotone in staffing is close to ours, and we present ours as supporting evidence. Turan (2026) studies approval gates for agent actions, with a risk-score threshold and a simulated reviewer whose reliability falls once the number of reviews passes a capacity; using 125 hand-labeled actions, the paper finds that safety peaks at an intermediate escalation rate. The author states that none of the mechanisms is new and that the results are simulations, not a human study. Alves et al. (2024) add workload constraints to cost-sensitive learning to defer: instances are assigned to a team of experts by constraint programming under capacity limits, and the method is tested on fraud detection with synthetic analysts.

On the queueing side, Naor (1969) showed that joining decisions made against the observed queue exceed the social optimum. Our result on hand-off rules applies this externality; it is an application and not a new principle. For the convexity of the mean queue size in the M/M/c queue we rely on Grassmann (1983), and Lee and Cohen (1983) treat related performance measures.

The decision to take a task back is also a stopping problem (Wald 1947; Shiryaev 1963). We use the monotone case of Chow, Robbins, and Siegmund (1971) to obtain a closed-form threshold. Which signal to monitor is a question of informativeness (Blackwell 1953; Holmström 1979), and our distinction between monitoring a process and verifying its output follows the control-portfolio view of Kirsch (1997).

Finally, benchmarks such as SWE-agent (Yang et al. 2024) and $\tau$-bench (Yao et al. 2024; Barres et al. 2025) report whether agents finish their tasks. They do not model the dynamics of errors within a run, and they do not derive decision rules for a firm that deploys an agent. Persistence in observed outcomes can arise because past outcomes change future behavior or because units differ (Heckman 1981). We address this with a model of task heterogeneity and, more decisively, with randomized injection of errors.
