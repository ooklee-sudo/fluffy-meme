Organizations increasingly attach small fine-tuned adapters to foundation models they license but do not control, and every new model release forces a choice: retrain each adapter, keep it as it is, or pay for a cheaper repair. The right choice depends on how much of an adapter survives an upgrade, a quantity that is rarely measured, which makes this a data science problem as much as an operational one. We formulate the choice as an optimal stopping problem with stochastic releases and value, which yields a closed-form retraining threshold and a price ceiling for any transfer method, both driven by the share of the retraining benefit that the cheaper option retains. We measure that share on 22 upgrades of open models with 0.5 to 1.7 billion parameters across three tasks, and we estimate release rates from the histories of six model families. Continued pre-training left copied adapters with 70% to nearly 100% of their benefit, whereas moving from a base model to an instruction-tuned one often destroyed them; tracing one family through its published post-training stages places the damage in supervised fine-tuning, not in later preference or reinforcement-learning updates. A label-free activation-drift measure ranked upgrades within a family without error but did not transfer across families, and a closed-form transfer method never repaid its cost. We learn that whether an upgrade changes the kind of model an adapter sits on matters more than how far the weights moved, and that recovery belongs to each adapter rather than to a release. For data science practice, maintenance of systems built on external foundation models can rest on a few inexpensive measurements, provided they are taken adapter by adapter and calibrated within a model family.
