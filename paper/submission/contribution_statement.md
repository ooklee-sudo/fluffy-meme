# Contribution Statement (draft for the ISR cover letter; limit 500 words)

**(a) Contribution to the literature.** Research on AI–human collaboration in information systems studies how people delegate to and learn from AI, and research on IT service contracting studies how fixed-price, time-and-materials and outcome-based contracts allocate risk between client and vendor. Generative AI services create a setting that neither literature addresses directly: output is stochastic and checkable, the same task can be attempted repeatedly, and the vendor can charge per attempt or per verified result. We model this as a vendor–buyer game in which the buyer chooses how many failed attempts to tolerate before escalating to a human, and defects differ in how easily they are repaired, so that every failure is informative about the next attempt.

The paper makes four contributions. First, it shows that the buyer's optimal escalation rule is a reservation threshold that holds for any distribution of repair difficulty, and that a homogeneous model, which ignores the information in failures, would prescribe never stopping. Second, it identifies an *incentive rent of sequential search*: because later attempts are less likely to succeed than earlier ones, any pay-per-use contract that induces deep sampling must leave the buyer a gain on the earlier attempts. This rent explains why a per-attempt price above cost makes buyers stop too early (double marginalization, applied to attempts), why a monopolist under-supplies sampling even under outcome-based pricing, why a pure success fee is the vendor's best pay-per-use contract, and why a lump-sum fee would remove all distortions. Third, it shows that private vendor quality reverses the profit order across contract forms, so that only outcome-based pricing can credibly signal quality, and that competition removes the distortion only when vendors are close substitutes, not merely numerous. Fourth, it tests the model's premises on open repair data (574 bugs, 45 models, plus a new 40-sample experiment) and calibrates costs from public wage statistics, task-time annotations and API price lists, showing where the conclusions hold (when verification is cheap relative to human repair) and where they weaken.

**(b) Influence on practice, policy and society.** Organizations are signing contracts for AI-assisted maintenance without a way to choose how long the AI should keep trying or how to read a vendor's price structure. The paper gives buyers an operational rule (estimate the dispersion of repair difficulty from their own logs, compute the threshold from their cost of human repair and the full cost of an attempt, and validate it on held-out defects), and it shows that pass@k benchmarks measure capability, not the economic decision. It gives vendors and buyers a basis for choosing between per-attempt and outcome-based pricing and for anticipating how market structure shifts the choice. For policy and standards bodies, it shows that the inputs the contracts depend on, per-sample outcomes and per-attempt costs, are not disclosed in current public leaderboards, which supports disclosure requirements in AI procurement. The analysis also helps interpret how AI services may shift routine maintenance work between machines and engineers, and who captures the resulting savings.
