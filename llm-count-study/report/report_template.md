## Abstract

LLM-based synthetic customers are proposed as a cheap way to pre-test marketing decisions, yet the distribution of per-customer purchase counts, which feeds customer lifetime value, repeat-purchase forecasts, and budget allocation, has rarely been checked. We compare four ways of eliciting 4-week purchase counts from three LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3.3-70B, Claude Haiku 5.5) against two public transaction data sets with very different means (UCI Online Retail II, mean about 0.5; dunnhumby Complete Journey, mean about 4.8), and we test whether the same models reproduce the effect of a randomized email promotion (Hillstrom, 2008). Real counts are strongly overdispersed (negative binomial, variance-to-mean ratios of 2.5 and 7.9 in our reference settings). Direct answers and day-by-day simulation collapse toward a single value in both settings; a design in which the model describes a distribution and an external generator samples from it recovers some of the dispersion but under-produces zeros; and a hybrid that lets the model set only a customer's mean and injects the remaining heterogeneity from an external gamma distribution matches the dispersion when the model's mean is right and fails when it is not. The promotion effect is positive in direction in most settings but its size varies by more than an order of magnitude with model and prompt, the absolute lift in purchase probability is overstated 4 to 26 times, and the models barely distinguish a men's from a women's email. We discuss implications for using synthetic customers in decision support.

## 1. Introduction

Synthetic customers (silicon samples) generated by large language models are increasingly proposed as a low-cost substitute for survey panels and for pre-launch marketing experiments. A repeatedly reported weakness is variance collapse: simulated responses cluster on the modal answer and are narrower than real ones. For count data this has a precise statistical meaning. Real purchase counts are overdispersed relative to a Poisson process because customers differ in their purchase rates, and a simulator that compresses customer heterogeneity should therefore appear first as underdispersion and a distorted share of zeros. The research questions are the following.

- **RQ1.** Does the per-period purchase count distribution of synthetic customers match the real one in mean, dispersion, zero proportion, and tail?
- **RQ2.** Does variance collapse appear in count data as underdispersion and a distorted zero proportion?
- **RQ3.** Do prompt and sampling designs change fidelity?
- **RQ4.** Does the promotion effect estimated with synthetic customers agree with a randomized experiment in direction and size?

## 3. Data and Design

### 3.1 Reference data

Table 1 summarizes the three reference data sets. For Online Retail II the simulation reference is a negative binomial distribution calibrated to the real 4-week counts of customers whose first purchase fell in the first three months (mean 0.49, variance-to-mean ratio 2.49, zero proportion 0.74, overdispersion alpha 3.05). Our re-aggregation of the public file gave a similar profile (mean 0.43 to 0.51, zero proportion 0.66 to 0.72, alpha 2.2 to 4.2 across seven consecutive windows, cohort of 1,731 customers); the Cameron and Trivedi test rejected the Poisson model in every window. For Complete Journey the reference is the empirical distribution of 4-week basket counts pooled over 13 windows and 2,469 loyalty-card households in 2017 (32,097 household-windows). For Hillstrom the real benchmark is the randomized difference between customers who received no email and those who received a men's or a women's email.

### 3.2 Synthetic customer designs

Each simulated customer is a short persona text (segment, age, and country or household size; for Hillstrom, the real covariates of a sampled customer). Four elicitation designs are compared.

- **A, Direct.** The persona is asked how many purchases (or shopping trips) it will make in the next 4 weeks and answers with an integer.
- **B, Daily.** For each of the four weeks the persona decides, for each of 7 days, whether it makes a purchase (one call per week); the daily indicators are summed.
- **C, Describe then sample.** The persona states probabilities for count bins (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5+ in the retail setting; 0, 1-2, 3-4, 5-7, 8-12, 13-20, 21+ in the grocery setting), and a count is drawn with an external random number generator. The top bin is truncated, so C cannot reach the full real variance: its attainable variance-to-mean ratio is 2.10 in the retail setting and 6.61 in the grocery setting.
- **D, Hybrid.** The persona gives only its expected mean count lambda. The count is Poisson with rate lambda times an external gamma multiplier. The gamma variance is set so that total heterogeneity matches the reference after accounting for the variation already contained in the model's lambdas, which avoids double counting between-segment variance.

Every design is run in two prompt variants: *plain*, and *anchored*, in which the persona is told the base purchase rate of the population (about 0.5 purchases per 4 weeks, most customers make none; about 5 shopping trips per 4 weeks, some make none).

### 3.3 Models, runs, and statistics

We used GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, and Claude Haiku 5.5 through the OpenRouter API. In the retail setting, GPT-4o-mini and Llama were run at temperatures 0.3, 0.7 and 1.0 and pooled (600 customers per cell); in the grocery and Hillstrom settings temperature was fixed at 0.7 (200 and 300 customers). Claude Haiku 5.5 does not accept a temperature setting, so it contributes one run per prompt variant, and its hidden reasoning was switched off so that short answers are not truncated. For each cell we report the mean, the variance-to-mean ratio, the zero proportion, and the overdispersion parameter alpha = (variance - mean) / mean squared, with 95% bootstrap intervals over customers. An asterisk marks an interval that excludes the reference value. For the email experiment we compare, in the same persona, the elicited purchase or visit probability under no email, a men's email, and a women's email (one independent call per arm), and report the risk ratio and the absolute lift.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Retail setting (mean about 0.5)

Table 2 and Figure 1a show that no direct or daily design reproduces the real dispersion. In all 18 cells of designs A, B and C the upper bound of the variance-to-mean interval is below the reference of 2.49. Direct answers are the most compressed (variance-to-mean ratio 0.00 to 0.57, excluding the degenerate all-zero cell). Under the anchored prompt Llama answered "0" and GPT-4o-mini answered "1" for practically every customer; in both cases the answer carries no persona information. Daily simulation overstates the mean without the anchor (GPT-4o-mini: 13.6 purchases in 28 days) and stays close to Poisson or below it. Describe-then-sample reaches 0.96 to 1.59, comparable to or above daily simulation, but remains below its own attainable ceiling of 2.10 in every cell, and its zero proportion is too low (0.32 to 0.68, against 0.74).

The hybrid design matches the reference variance-to-mean ratio in three of six cells (both Claude Haiku cells and GPT-4o-mini plain), but this agreement is partly by construction because the gamma multiplier injects the dispersion. What the model contributes is the mean, and the means differ: Llama's anchored mean is twice the reference (1.00 against 0.49).

### 4.2 Grocery setting (mean about 4.8)

A higher mean does not relieve the collapse of direct answers (Table 3, Figure 1b). Variance-to-mean ratios of design A lie between 0.00 and 0.27 against a reference of 7.85; GPT-4o-mini and Llama answered "8" for practically every customer without the anchor, and GPT-4o-mini answered "5" for everyone with it. Daily simulation again overstates the mean (7.1 to 16.6 trips against 4.84) and has almost no dispersion. In 17 of the 18 cells of designs A, B and C the variance-to-mean interval lies entirely below the reference.

Describe-then-sample behaves better here. With the anchor, the intervals of all three models contain the largest attainable value (6.61), and the dispersion parameter (0.80 to 0.96) approaches the attainable 1.18; the plain variants stay below it. Its weak point is the zero proportion: 0.01 to 0.12 against the real 0.19. The hybrid again matches the reference dispersion in four of six cells, but its mean is unstable (2.94 for Haiku with the anchor, 5.80 for GPT-4o-mini without it).

### 4.3 Randomized email experiment (Hillstrom)

Table 4 reports the elicited-probability design (the most stable of the three formats we tried) and Figure 2 the absolute lift for the men's email. The real effect is a risk ratio of 1.72 for visits and 2.19 for purchases (men's email), with lifts of 7.7 and 0.7 percentage points.

![Figure 2. Absolute lift in the probability of a visit and of a purchase after a men's email (elicited-probability design). Bars are 95% intervals; the dashed line and band are the real lift and its 95% interval. Note the logarithmic axis in panel b.](fig2_lift.png){width=6.4in}

Four patterns emerge. First, the direction is mostly reproduced: the risk ratio exceeds one in all 12 probability-elicitation cells, but it is essentially one for GPT-4o-mini with the anchor for visits (1.01 and 1.06; the model answered about 10% regardless of the email), and Llama with the anchor gave a risk ratio below one (0.63) in the yes/no format for visits. Second, the size is unstable. For visits, Llama and Haiku without the anchor give lifts of +0.070 and +0.078, close to the real +0.077, but GPT-4o-mini gives +0.199 and Haiku with the anchor +0.159. Third, the absolute lift in purchase probability is overstated in every setting, by about 4 to 26 times (for example +0.180 for GPT-4o-mini without the anchor, against a real +0.0068). Providing the base rate lowers the no-email purchase probability toward the real 0.57% for Llama and Haiku but leaves the email-arm probability high, so the risk ratio explodes (16 to 45) while the absolute lift stays large. Fourth, the models do not reliably separate the men's from the women's email. In reality the men's email has the larger lift (+0.077 against +0.045 for visits, a gap of +0.031 with interval +0.024 to +0.038). In 10 of the 12 probability-elicitation cells the interval of the simulated gap includes zero; in the other two (GPT-4o-mini with the anchor) the women's email is rated higher, the opposite of the real result. Direct yes/no answers are not usable for rare events: without an email GPT-4o-mini answered "No" for every customer and with an email about half answered "Yes", so the risk ratio is undefined, and the same happened with Llama and Haiku for purchases in several cells. In absolute terms the yes/no purchase lifts range from +0.14 to +0.55, 21 to 81 times the real lift.

## 5. Discussion

| Hypothesis | Evidence in this draft |
|---|---|
| H1. Naive persona simulation has a smaller variance-to-mean ratio and distorted zero proportion. | Supported. 35 of 36 cells of designs A, B and C across the two settings lie below the reference variance-to-mean ratio. |
| H2. LLM counts are Poisson-like or narrower. | Supported for A and B in both settings and for C in the retail setting. Not supported for C with the anchor in the grocery setting, whose dispersion is within reach of its attainable ceiling. |
| H3. A hybrid with externally injected heterogeneity beats pure LLM designs. | Partly supported. It matches dispersion in 7 of 12 cells, but the dispersion is injected, and its accuracy depends on the model's mean, which the anchor sometimes worsens. |
| H4. Treatment effects keep their direction but their size is over- or under-estimated. | Partly supported. Direction mostly holds; simulated risk ratios range from 1.0 to 45 against real values of 1.7 and 2.2, and the absolute lift in purchases is overstated 4 to 26 times. |
| H5. Direct responses are underdispersed because low means are rounded to 0, 1, 2. | Not supported in this form. Direct answers collapse at a mean of 0.5 and of 4.8 alike; collapse is not specific to low means. |

Three practical implications follow. First, a decision that depends on the shape of the count distribution (lifetime value, heavy-buyer share) cannot rely on direct or day-by-day synthetic answers, and describing a distribution helps only partly. Second, giving the model the base rate is not a general fix: it corrects the mean in some designs, collapses all answers to one value in others, and changes the size of the promotion effect by an order of magnitude. Third, relative effects (risk ratios) and absolute effects can lead to opposite conclusions when the simulated base rate is wrong, so both should be reported and validated against real base rates before any revenue calculation.

## 6. Limitations

- One random seed, 200 to 600 simulated customers per cell, one prompt wording per design, and three small models; intervals do not capture variation across prompts, personas, or seeds.
- The Retail II reference is a calibrated parametric distribution rather than the raw data, and the Complete Journey households are loyalty-card shoppers with relatively high purchase frequency.
- Personas are simple and their attributes were drawn independently; the Complete Journey demographics were not used. In the email experiment the real customer covariates were used.
- In the Complete Journey data campaigns are targeted, not randomized, so only the Hillstrom experiment supports causal comparison of treatment effects. The three arms of the email experiment are elicited within the same persona through independent calls, which mimics, but does not equal, random assignment.
- Design C truncates its top bin and therefore cannot reach the real variance; this was quantified (attainable ratios 2.10 and 6.61) but is a design limitation. Public data may be part of the models' training data.

## 7. Next steps

1. Repeat the runs with several seeds, additional prompt wordings (for example verbalized-sampling prompts), and persona attributes taken from the real demographic tables; add confidence intervals for the absolute email lifts.
2. Test larger and more recent models to see whether the collapse and the overstated purchase lift persist.
3. Obtain a randomized promotion data set with purchase counts (for example from a partner firm) to evaluate the count-distribution and the treatment-effect questions on the same customers.
4. Refine the hybrid design so that the model's mean is calibrated on a small pilot, and evaluate it as a method contribution.
