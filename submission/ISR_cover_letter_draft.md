Dear Editors,

We submit "Acquired, Not Yet Assimilated? Complementary Assets and the Limits of Occupation-Level Evidence on Generative Visual AI" for consideration at *Information Systems Research*.

**Summary.** Statistics that show firms have adopted generative visual AI are often read as evidence that creative work is being displaced. We argue that this reading skips a step that IS research has long emphasized: the gap between acquiring a technology and assimilating it into work. We develop a task-level model in which complementary assets enter through the cost of reviewing machine output, show why near-zero acquisition cost should widen the gap, and ask what aggregate public data can reveal about it.

## Contribution statement

**1. Theory.** We extend assimilation theory (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005) to a technology whose acquisition costs almost nothing. Earlier assimilation research studied technologies that were costly to acquire, so acquisition itself partly selected firms that had already built complements. We locate complementary assets (expertise, review capacity, compliance procedures) in a single observable cost term, review cost, and derive two implications: when review cost exceeds the cost of human production, no improvement in the model triggers assimilation, and cheap acquisition lets adoption statistics grow while the share of acquirers that assimilate falls. The first implication rests on an assumption that we state explicitly (review hours do not fall with model quality) and discuss as a limitation.

**2. Method.** We derive a detectability bound for exposure-based hiring designs, the coefficient that complete assimilation would produce per standard deviation of exposure, γ* = g·A·β·σ_s·√R, compared with the minimum detectable effect. The bound separates designs that can speak to assimilation from designs that cannot. We apply it to our own European and Canadian job-advert data and to nine published studies. Occupation- and industry-level designs with aggregate outcomes cannot detect platform-sized effects; designs with microdata or observed adoption can. We provide a calculator, a derivation, and simulation checks in the replication materials.

**3. Evidence.** Using Eurostat data, 9.6 percent of EU enterprises (36 percent in publishing, film, and broadcasting) report using generative visual AI, and non-adopters cite missing expertise (70 percent) and legal uncertainty (54 percent) far more often than lack of usefulness (18 percent). Occupation-level advert data show small exposure gradients that cannot be distinguished from earlier trends. Advert text shows that only about 3 percent of visual-production postings in 2024 mention generative AI tools and about 0.2 percent require them.

**What we do not claim.** We do not observe assimilation inside firms, and our attempt to test the firm-level hypothesis with public advert data does not identify the mechanism (we report that attempt, including a failed placebo test, in Section 7.6). We do not claim that generative visual AI has no labor-market effects. The manuscript specifies the firm-level data that would test the assimilation-gap account.

## Fit with the journal

The manuscript builds on two lines of work published in this journal: the assimilation gap (Fichman & Kemerer, 1999) and post-adoption variation in usage and value (Zhu & Kraemer, 2005), and it uses online job-advert data in the spirit of Tambe & Hitt (2012). It connects them to current IS work on managing AI (Berente et al., 2021) and on human–AI delegation (Fügener et al., 2022), and to labor-economics studies of AI exposure that IS readers increasingly rely on.
