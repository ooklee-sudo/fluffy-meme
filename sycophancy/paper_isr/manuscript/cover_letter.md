Dear Editor-in-Chief,

I am pleased to submit the manuscript "When Should an AI Reviewer Back Down? Pushback, Verification, and Deference in AI-Assisted Code Review" for consideration as a Research Article in *Information Systems Research*. The paper develops and tests an economic account of when an AI system should change its verdict after a person disputes it, a question that arises wherever organizations delegate review, recommendation, or approval to generative AI. It combines analytical models, controlled code-review experiments with ten language models, structural estimation, two field data sets, a policy simulation, and an out-of-sample confirmatory test. The intended audience is IS researchers working on human–AI interaction and the economics of IS, as well as practitioners who configure and govern AI review systems.

# Contribution Statement

**Contribution to scholarship.** IS research has studied at length when people defer to algorithms. With conversational AI, influence also runs the other way: people dispute the system, and the system must decide whether to defer. This paper provides a normative account of that decision. A Bayesian receiver benchmark defines sycophancy and stubbornness precisely and separates the legitimate effect of reconsideration from social pressure. A strategic-user model shows that, once the people who object benefit from a changed verdict, deference becomes an equilibrium object: cheap objections unravel, optimal deference is interior, a small excess destroys the information in every objection, and evidence requirements pay off only within a window of fabrication costs. Because current models violate the benchmark, we develop an ambiguity-averse reviewer whose parameters can be estimated from behavior, and we prove its main properties. The empirical work shows that models ignore stated reliability, respond to the form rather than the content of objections, accept fabricated evidence, and differ in type, and that these regularities replicate out of sample on new items and new models. Field data from developer–ChatGPT conversations and 39,592 replies to AI review bots on GitHub show that disputes are common in practice and that deployed bots concede to about 80% of them. Together, these results reframe sycophancy from a model defect into a problem of design and governance, linking the IS literatures on algorithmic advice and delegation to the economics of cheap talk, verification, and ambiguity.

**Contribution to practice and policy.** The policy simulation, run on the measured responses of each model, gives concrete guidance to organizations that deploy AI reviewers. Honoring unsupported objections by default raises expected loss for most models. Evidence requirements help only when fabrication is costly, and pairing them with verification aimed at the disputed part of a claim lowers that threshold. Deference should be set per system and per direction of claim, because the same policy can help one system and harm another. Finally, frontier models with reasoning switched on are nearly immune to pushback but cost 16 to 26 times as much per exchange, so organizations face an explicit trade-off between paying for reasoning and pairing inexpensive reviewers with a deference policy and verification. These findings bear on the procurement, configuration, and governance of AI systems in software development and in other settings where interested parties can argue with an automated decision.
